# PART 0 — HEADLINE FINDING (read this first)

**The single-subject rule does NOT invalidate the omnibus, and it cannot be used as the argument for splitting it.** The opposite is true: Utah has one of the most permissive single-subject doctrines in the country, and the Utah Supreme Court has said in terms that **"there is no constitutional restriction as to the *scope or magnitude* of the single subject of a legislative act."**

I went looking for the finding the project wanted — "the omnibus is unconstitutional on single-subject grounds, therefore splitting is legally required." That finding is not available. Asserting it would be false, and a legislator's counsel would puncture it in one phone call to the Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel. Do not use it.

**But the research produced something better, and it is genuinely load-bearing.** Utah Constitution Article XXIII, Section 1, paragraph 2 says that a proposed **new Article** of the Utah Constitution:

> "may relate to **one subject, or any number of subjects**, and may modify, or repeal provisions contained in other articles of the Constitution, if such provisions are germane to the subject matter of the article being revised, amended or being proposed as a new article."

That is a constitutional grant of exactly the breadth Operation Checkmate needs — and it exists **only** in the constitutional-amendment vehicle, not in the statutory-bill vehicle. Meanwhile the one thing in the current draft that is *unambiguously and fatally* in the wrong container is **§15, the constitutional amendment, which is currently riding inside a statutory bill**. A statute cannot amend a constitution. That is not a policy objection, not a drafting preference, and not a matter of degree. It is a hard structural fact with a clean fix.

**The non-ideological argument for restructure is therefore this, and it is airtight:**

> The bill is not too big. It is in the wrong *containers*. The constitutional architecture has to travel as a Joint Resolution under Art. XXIII § 1 — where Utah law expressly permits *any number of subjects*. The operative offenses have to travel as statutes. Right now they are fused, and the fusion is what kills it, because the fused object can be passed by neither route.

That framing loses nothing the author wants. It actually gives him *more* scope for the structural core than he currently has, because Art. XXIII § 1 lets a new Article carry unlimited subjects and repeal conflicting provisions elsewhere in the constitution — which is precisely what §15's entrenchment ambition was reaching for.

# PART 1 — THE SINGLE-SUBJECT QUESTION, SETTLED

## 1.1 The text (verified verbatim)

**Utah Const. art. VI, § 22** — *Reading of bills — Bill to contain only one subject — Bills passed by majority*:

> "Every bill shall be read by title three separate times in each house except in cases where two-thirds of the house where such bill is pending suspend this requirement. **Except general appropriation bills and bills for the codification and general revision of laws, no bill shall be passed containing more than one subject, which shall be clearly expressed in its title.** The vote upon the final passage of all bills shall be by yeas and nays and entered upon the respective journals of the house in which the vote occurs. No bill or joint resolution shall be passed except with the assent of the majority of all the members elected to each house of the Legislature."

Source: https://ballotpedia.org/Article_VI,_Utah_Constitution (cross-checked against the text quoted at ¶ 26 of *Gregory v. Shurtleff*).

Note the fourth sentence: passage requires a majority of **all members elected**, not a majority of those voting. In the House that is 38 of 75; in the Senate 15 of 29. Absences count as no votes. This matters for floor strategy.

## 1.2 The controlling case

***Gregory v. Shurtleff*, 2013 UT 18, 299 P.3d 1098.** I read the full slip opinion (Utah Supreme Court, Nos. 20110277 / 20110473, filed March 19, 2013; Durham, J., for the Court; Lee, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part, joined by Durrant, C.J.). PDF: https://cases.justia.com/utah/supreme-court/20110277.pdf

This is the modern controlling authority and the Court said so — at ¶ 29 it noted the appellants "caused this court to consider those rules and clarify the standards they impose **for the first time in decades**."

**Facts, which are directly on point for our question.** Senate Bill 2 (2008) bundled **fourteen separate education measures** — new programs, amendments to existing programs, *and* appropriations — into one bill. Some of the fourteen had been **defeated on the House floor as standalone bills**; others had **died in committee**; the challengers alleged they were revived by "bundling" so they could "ride 'piggy-back' on popular legislation and money measures to enactment at the eleventh hour" (¶ 49).

**Held: no violation of either the single-subject rule or the clear-title rule.** ¶ 10, ¶ 62.

## 1.3 The test, in the Court's own words

**Liberal construction, expressly.** ¶ 40, quoting *Salt Lake City v. Wilson*, 148 P. 1104, 1109 (Utah 1915):

> the rule "is mandatory and binding alike upon the courts and the Legislature, yet **it should be liberally construed in favor of upholding a law**, and should be so applied as to effectuate its purpose in preventing the combination of **incongruous subjects neither of which could be passed when standing alone**. **A too strict application of the provision might, however, result in hampering wholesome legislation upon any comprehensive subject rather than in preventing evils.**"

**Size is expressly irrelevant.** ¶ 40, quoting *Martineau v. Crabbe*, 150 P. 301, 304 (Utah 1915) (itself quoting *State v. Morgan*, 48 N.W. 314, 317 (N.D. 1891)):

> "'[T]here is **no constitutional restriction as to the scope or magnitude** of the single subject of a legislative act.'"

**Comprehensive measures are affirmatively permitted.** ¶ 40, quoting *Kent Club v. Toronto*, 305 P.2d 870, 873 (Utah 1957):

> "A liberal view should be taken of both the act and the constitutional provisions so as not to hamper the law making power, but **to permit the adoption of comprehensive measures covering a whole subject**."

**No formula, and deliberately so.** ¶ 42: "We have never established, and do not create today, a precise formula for determining whether a challenged act 'contain[s] more than one subject.' Such a formula may well be impossible to craft, and might be undesirable even if it were possible."

**What a violation actually looks like.** ¶ 43 — the Court's own illustration of a bill that "all would agree" violates the rule is "a bill dealing with **pet licenses, mining regulation and beekeeping**." The standard is *total incongruity*, not breadth. The Court noted approvingly at ¶ 44 n.23 that some states "set the bar for what constitutes a violation of the single-subject rule at the level of **total incongruity**," citing *People v. Cervantes*, 723 N.E.2d 265, 267 (Ill. 1999) ("unrelated provisions that **by no fair interpretation** have any legitimate relation to one another").

**Volume of provisions is not a problem.** ¶ 44: "under our tradition of liberal construction a bill addressing even a relatively large number of educational programs does not violate that rule," citing with approval *Akin v. Dir. of Revenue*, 934 S.W.2d 295, 302 (Mo. 1996) (bill with **twenty enactments and thirty-eight reenactments** plus revenue mechanisms = one subject, "education").

**Mixing substantive law with money is not per se a violation.** ¶¶ 45–48 — the challengers asked the Court to adopt the bright-line rule of *Washington State Legislature v. State*, 985 P.2d 353 (Wash. 1999). The Court **declined**: "We are unpersuaded ... We therefore decline to adopt such a rule" (¶ 48).

**Log-rolling history is off-limits.** ¶ 50: "the text of Article VI, Section 22 speaks to the contents and title of the Bill itself; **it makes no reference to legislative motive**." ¶ 51: no prior Utah opinion analyzes a single-subject claim by reference to legislative history. ¶ 52: "the line between forbidden 'log-rolling' and mere 'horse-trading' may be a fine one, and we are not confident in our ability — or even our constitutional power — to police it."

**Invalidation is rare and the Court knows it.** ¶ 43 n.22, quoting Richard Briffault, *The Item Veto in State Courts*, 66 Temp. L. Rev. 1171, 1177–78 (1993): "**The invalidation of a state law for a violation of the single-subject rule is a rarity.**"

## 1.4 The clear-title rule — and why Utah's long title is a *feature* here

¶¶ 53–61. SB 2's short title was five words: "MINIMUM SCHOOL PROGRAM BUDGET AMENDMENTS." The Court held that by itself it "might well be **unconstitutionally under-inclusive**" (¶ 60) — but that the **LONG TITLE** (a "General Description" plus a bullet-pointed list of "Highlighted Provisions") **is the constitutional "title"** for Art. VI § 22 purposes, and it cured the defect.

¶ 59 is the sentence to remember:

> "As we have interpreted and applied it above, the single-subject rule permits one Bill to treat multiple aspects of the public education system. Accordingly, **a title such as the one this Bill has is arguably the fairest way of putting legislators and citizens on notice of what the Bill contains.** If the Bill's contents are constitutional in scope — and we have determined that they are — then **an itemized description of them is a constitutionally acceptable way to 'clearly express[]' those contents**."

Governing standards for clear title:
- *Thomas v. Daughters of Utah Pioneers*, 197 P.2d 477, 508 (Utah 1948) (Latimer, J., concurring), quoted at ¶ 55: a title is sufficient "if it is not productive of surprise and fraud and is not calculated to mislead the legislature or the people, but is of such character as fairly to apprise the legislators and the public of the subject matter of the legislation and to put anyone having an interest in the subject on inquiry."
- *Jensen v. Matheson*, 583 P.2d 77, 80 (Utah 1978), quoted at ¶ 55: the purpose is that "the legislators will be advised of the subject and purpose of the act in order that there be **no misunderstanding, omitting, nor burying or obscuring** of what is being proposed."
- *Marioneaux v. Cutler*, 91 P. 355, 359 (Utah 1907), ¶ 60 n.30: where "a reasonable doubt exist[s] ... whether the subject of the act is expressed in the title, [] such doubt will be resolved in favor of the act."
- *State ex rel. Edler v. Edwards*, 95 P. 367, 369 (Utah 1908), ¶ 60: extraneous surplus matter in a title is "harmless."

**Operational consequence for Checkmate:** the clear-title rule does not bar the bill. It imposes a *drafting obligation* — the long title must itemize every offense created and every code section affected. For a bill creating 120+ named offenses that is a very long long-title. That is a cost in drafting hours, not a constitutional bar, and per ¶ 59 it is affirmatively the safest course.

## 1.5 The only two Utah laws actually struck under § 22 — both incongruity, neither size

1. **Clear title:** *Ritchie v. Richards*, 47 P. 670, 674 (Utah 1896) (¶ 54). Act titled "An act relating to and making sundry provisions concerning **elections**" could not carry a provision governing **appointment** of persons to vacated positions. "This section does not relate to elections, nor does it concern elections. Therefore the title does not embrace it."
2. **Single subject:** *Carter v. State Tax Commission*, 96 P.2d 727, 733–34 (Utah 1939) (¶ 46). A provision graduating motor-vehicle registration fees by **type of fuel** was struck because fuel type "bears **no relationship** to the object of the legislation." The Court in *Gregory* was explicit that *Carter* turned on **incongruity**, not on the provision being a revenue measure.

Other merits cases in the line, cited at ¶ 24: *State v. Barlow*, 153 P.2d 647, 655 (Utah 1944); *Pass v. Kanell*, 100 P.2d 972 (Utah 1940); *State v. Edwards*, 95 P. 367 (Utah 1908). And *Laney v. Fairview City*, 2002 UT 79, ¶ 34, 57 P.3d 1007, quoted at ¶ 26 for the purpose of § 22: to "limit legislative power and prevent special interest abuse," "clearly motivated by a wariness of unlimited legislative power."

## 1.6 So: does the 130,000-word omnibus violate Art. VI § 22?

**On the single-subject clause: almost certainly not.** Checkmate has one identifiable subject — *the conduct of government actors exercising public power over citizens, and the remedies for abuse of that power*. Every listed component (criminal offenses, civil remedies, immunity, due process duties, forfeiture, nullity) is germane to that subject. Under *Martineau* magnitude is irrelevant; under *Kent Club* comprehensive measures covering a whole subject are affirmatively permitted; under *Gregory* ¶ 44 a large number of provisions within one subject is fine. This is not close.

**On the clear-title clause: not, provided the long title itemizes.** Per ¶¶ 56–61. The risk is under-inclusiveness, and the cure is itemization.

**The three real § 22-adjacent exposures, each an engineering problem with a fix:**

| # | Exposure | Why | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **§15 constitutional amendment inside a statutory bill** | A statute cannot amend a constitution. Art. XXIII § 1 requires **two-thirds of all members elected to each house**, journal entry with yeas and nays, newspaper publication **in every county** for **two months** before the next general election, and **majority voter ratification**. Art. VI § 22 passes bills by simple majority of members elected. The two cannot be satisfied by one instrument. | Sever §15 entirely into a **separate Joint Resolution (SJR/HJR)**. Non-negotiable, and independent of any policy view. |
| 2 | **Outlier provisions vulnerable to *Carter* incongruity** | Provisions whose connection to "government accountability" a hostile court could call attenuated — e.g. §17 Subpart E (federal representatives / intergovernmental), Election Authority Abuse, Public Health Emergency Authority Abuse. | Low risk, and the remedy under *Carter* is **severance of the outlier**, not death of the act. §13 severability already covers it. Additionally, tie each such offense's operative text back to the defined term "Government Actor" so germaneness is textual, not inferential. |
| 3 | **Under-inclusive short title** | *Gregory* ¶ 60: a short title alone "might well be unconstitutionally under-inclusive." | Draft the full Utah **long title** convention: "General Description," "Highlighted Provisions" (itemized bullets), "Money Appropriated in this Bill," "Other Special Clauses," "Utah Code Sections Affected." OLRGC does this as a matter of course. |

## 1.7 The finding that changes the strategy: Art. XXIII § 1

**Utah Const. art. XXIII, § 1 (verified verbatim** — https://ballotpedia.org/Article_XXIII,_Utah_Constitution**):**

> "Any amendment or amendments to this Constitution may be proposed in either house of the Legislature, and if **two-thirds of all the members elected to each of the two houses** shall vote in favor thereof, such proposed amendment or amendments shall be entered on their respective journals with the yeas and nays taken thereon; and the Legislature shall cause the same to be **published in at least one newspaper in every county of the state**, where a newspaper is published, **for two months immediately preceding the next general election**, at which time the said amendment or amendments shall be **submitted to the electors** of the state for their approval or rejection, and if **a majority of the electors voting thereon** shall approve the same, such amendment or amendments shall become part of this Constitution.
>
> The revision or amendment of an **entire article** or the **addition of a new article** to this Constitution may be proposed as a single amendment and may be submitted to the electors as a single question or proposition. Such amendment **may relate to one subject, or any number of subjects**, and **may modify, or repeal provisions contained in other articles of the Constitution**, if such provisions are germane to the subject matter of the article being revised, amended or being proposed as a new article."

**Read the second paragraph again.** It is a standing constitutional authorization to do, in a proposed new Article, exactly what Art. VI § 22 forbids in a bill: cover any number of subjects, and reach into and repeal other parts of the constitution. Utah wrote a breadth exemption into its amendment power.

**What this buys the project:**

- The structural core — abolition of judicial, prosecutorial, qualified, and sovereign immunity as matters of Utah constitutional law; the personal-accountability principle; the citizen-enforcement authority — can be proposed as **a new Article of the Utah Constitution** covering *any number of subjects*, in one question to the voters.
- Put in the constitution, immunity abrogation is beyond the reach of an ordinary future legislature. That is what §15's entrenchment clause was trying to achieve, achieved by a mechanism that actually works. **The entrenchment clause becomes unnecessary and should be dropped** — it is also the provision most likely to draw a fatal objection, since one amendment cannot restrict the people's Art. XXIII power to adopt the next one.
- The route runs *through the voters*, not around them. For a bill whose entire moral premise is Art. I § 2 ("All political power is inherent in the people ... they have the right to alter or reform their government as the public welfare may require" — verified verbatim), a statewide ratification vote is not a concession. It is the thesis.
- Drop §15's claim of supremacy over federal law within Utah. It collides with U.S. Const. art. VI and it is the single easiest thing for an opponent to lead with. Nothing in the accountability architecture depends on it: a state may always give *more* protection under its own constitution, which is what Utah already does.

## 1.8 One more finding with two edges: citizens have standing to sue over § 22

*Gregory* ¶¶ 25–32 held that **public-interest standing exists for Art. VI § 22 claims** — a Utah citizen with no personal injury can sue to enforce the single-subject and clear-title rules, because those restrictions "are part of the fundamental structure of legislative power" (¶ 27).

- **For us:** confirms Utah's citizen-standing tradition is real and broad, which supports the Citizen Council premise generally. Pair with *Jenkins v. Swan*, 675 P.2d 1145 (Utah 1983) and *Cedar Mountain Envtl., Inc. v. Tooele Cnty.*, 2009 UT 48, ¶ 8, 214 P.3d 95.
- **Against us:** the day this passes, any opponent can sue on § 22 grounds without showing injury. That is a reason to make the long title bulletproof and itemized, not a reason to shrink the bill.
- **Caution:** Justice Lee's partial dissent (¶¶ 63–113, joined by Chief Justice Durrant) would have rejected public-interest standing entirely. Do not overclaim this doctrine as settled and unanimous.

## 1.9 The retroactivity problem — this one *is* a hard constraint

**Utah Const. art. I, § 18 (verified verbatim):** "No bill of attainder, ex post facto law, or law impairing the obligation of contracts shall be passed."

- **Retroactive criminal liability is flatly barred** — by Art. I § 18 and by U.S. Const. art. I, § 10. TLDR.md already states criminal penalties apply prospectively. Good. Make sure the bill text says so explicitly and unmistakably in §10 itself, not only in the summary, because §10 is where an opponent will look.
- **Retroactive civil liability (§10) plus no limitations period** is the more exposed piece. It runs into Utah's presumption against retroactive application and into vested-rights analysis under Art. I § 11 (Open Courts — verified verbatim: "All courts shall be open, and every person, for an injury done to the person in his or her person, property or reputation, shall have remedy by due course of law, which shall be administered without denial or unnecessary delay ..."). **Fix, preserving intent:** express retroactivity language plus a defined **revival window** (e.g. claims for prior conduct must be filed within N years of the effective date) rather than perpetual retroactive exposure. A revival window is a recognized device, survives review far better, and costs the author almost nothing — the conduct he cares about is still reachable.
- Also note **Art. I § 24** (verified): "All laws of a general nature shall have uniform operation." And **Art. I § 27** (verified): "Frequent recurrence to fundamental principles is essential to the security of individual rights and the perpetuity of free government" — a genuinely useful Utah-specific rhetorical anchor, and it is *in the constitution*, not invented.

What follows is its structural reasoning where it holds up, corrected against the primary sources I verified, with everything I could not confirm marked.

**Verified process facts:**
- **45 calendar days.** Utah Const. art. VI, § 16(1): "no annual general session of the Legislature may exceed 45 calendar days, excluding state holidays and federal holidays." Session convenes in January.
- **Three readings by title in each house**, unless two-thirds of the house suspends. Art. VI § 22.
- **Passage = majority of all members elected to each house** (38/75 House, 15/29 Senate). Art. VI § 22.
- **Constitutional amendment = two-thirds of all members elected to each house + county-by-county newspaper publication for two months + majority of electors voting.** Art. XXIII § 1.

**UNVERIFIED (check before repeating):** the exact per-legislator bill-file limit and its rule number; the specific Joint/House/Senate Rule numbers for fiscal notes and committee referral; the statutory cite making OLRGC drafting mandatory; the veto-override subsection of Art. VII § 8. These are all real constraints — I simply could not confirm the citations this session (the web-search budget was exhausted and le.utah.gov's code pages are JavaScript-rendered).

### Rank 1 — Wrong vehicle: a constitutional amendment inside a statute. Near-certain kill.
**Mechanism.** §15 cannot be enacted by bill. The moment OLRGC or any committee attorney sees it, the entire package is flagged as facially defective, and the defect contaminates the reader's judgment of everything else in the document. This is the one failure mode that requires no political opposition at all — it happens at intake, silently, on legal grounds nobody will argue with.
**Countermeasure.** Sever §15 into a standalone Joint Resolution. Sequence it *after* at least one statutory win, because 2/3 of both chambers plus a statewide ballot is the highest bar in Utah law and you want a track record before you ask for it. Drop the entrenchment clause and the federal-supremacy clause; Art. XXIII § 1 already supplies the entrenchment, and the second paragraph gives you the breadth.

### Rank 2 — Drafting capacity and the citizen-draft problem. Near-certain kill as filed.
**Mechanism.** Utah bills are drafted by the Office of Legislative Research and General Counsel to a rigid house style — long title, General Description, Highlighted Provisions, Money Appropriated, Other Special Clauses, Utah Code Sections Affected, with every amended section reproduced showing insertions and deletions. A 130,000-word citizen manuscript is not a bill; it is source material for one. Converting it is an enormous attorney-hour draw on an office that serves the entire legislature and has a queue that opens in the interim and closes before session.
**Countermeasure.** Do the conversion work *outside* the office and hand the sponsor something that is already 90% in house style. Deliver a lead bill of a size OLRGC can actually process — target 20–40 pages, not 500. Retain a former OLRGC or Utah legislative counsel to do the conversion if funds permit; this is the single highest-leverage dollar in the project. Open the bill file during the **interim (May–November 2026)**, not in January.

### Rank 3 — The 45-day clock. Near-certain kill for a package this size.
**Mechanism.** 45 calendar days, three readings by title in each chamber, committee hearings in both, fiscal note, floor calendars, and a last-two-weeks bottleneck dominated by the budget. A bill that needs multiple long committee hearings to be *understood* — before anyone even argues about it — cannot clear that gauntlet. Length is not the objection; **hearing-time consumption** is.
**Countermeasure.** Sequence across sessions: 2027 lead bill, 2028 second bill, 2029 joint resolution. Use the **2026 interim** to get the concept in front of an interim committee, where there is no clock and the discussion is substantive rather than procedural. A favorable interim airing is worth more than an early bill number.

### Rank 4 — Sponsor bandwidth and leadership pre-clearance. Very high.
**Mechanism.** Utah legislators are part-time and limited in how many bills they can open (**exact limit UNVERIFIED**). A bill this size consumes not one slot but the sponsor's entire session — every hearing, every amendment, every floor colloquy. Leadership vets what members carry. A sponsor who takes this on as filed is choosing to have no other legislative year.
**Countermeasure.** Make the ask *small and specific*. Never ask a legislator to "carry Operation Checkmate." Ask them to carry **one named bill** with a defined page count and a defined fiscal note. Distribute the series across multiple sponsors in both chambers so no single member absorbs the whole political cost. Recruit a committee chair for the bill that lands in that chair's committee.

### Rank 5 — Fiscal note. Very high, and it is the objection that kills quietly.
**Mechanism.** The Office of the Legislative Fiscal Analyst prepares a fiscal note before floor consideration. A bill that abolishes immunities, creates 120+ offenses, bars indemnification, creates a new adjudicatory body, and applies retroactively with no limitations period will come back either with a very large number or, worse, marked **indeterminate**. In a balanced-budget state, "indeterminate" on a controversial bill is a burial permit — it lets leadership decline to calendar it without ever taking a position.
**Countermeasure.** Three moves. (a) Draft the lead bill **appropriation-free** — authorize, don't appropriate. (b) Get in front of the LFA during the interim so the analyst is not seeing it cold in February. (c) **Reframe the fiscal argument, because the facts favor you**: §11's bar on indemnification *moves* liability off the public fisc onto individual wrongdoers. Utah cities and counties currently pay misconduct settlements out of public money. A bill that makes the wrongdoer pay is a taxpayer-protection bill. Lead with that, with real settlement figures.

### Rank 6 — Hostile committee referral. High.
**Mechanism.** Referral is a leadership prerogative. Law-enforcement-adjacent bills routinely land in committees populated by members with law-enforcement backgrounds and close ties to sheriffs' and chiefs' associations. A chair who does not want a hearing simply does not agenda it, or agendas it last on the final possible day.
**Countermeasure.** Draft each bill so it *cleanly* fits a preferred committee's jurisdiction, then confirm the likely referral before filing. Where the natural referral is hostile, frame the lead bill as a **judiciary / civil-remedies** matter rather than a law-enforcement matter — the immunity core genuinely is a civil-remedies question, and that framing is honest, not evasive. Recruit the vice-chair as a co-sponsor.

### Rank 7 — Rules Committee burial. High.
**Mechanism.** Rules controls the calendar. A bill can pass committee and simply never be calendared for third reading. There is no discharge mechanism available to a citizen advocate.
**Countermeasure.** Speed. Get out of the originating chamber in the first three weeks. Bills that arrive at Rules late with a controversial note die there by default. Bills already through one chamber by week three have momentum that Rules is reluctant to visibly spend capital to stop.

### Rank 8 — Constitutional review posture (OLRGC and the Attorney General). Moderate-to-high, and mostly about §15 and §10.
**Mechanism.** Legislative counsel flags constitutional problems for the sponsor before introduction. A note flagging the constitution-amended-by-statute defect, the retroactivity problem, and the federal-supremacy clause will cause most sponsors to walk. Note also that the Attorney General's office is not a neutral referee here — it **defends** state officers in exactly the suits this bill enables. Say that respectfully, but know it.
**Countermeasure.** Pre-fix the three known defects (§15 vehicle, retroactivity window, supremacy clause) before anyone sees the draft. Then the constitutional note is short, and a short note is a *sponsor's asset*, not a liability.

### Rank 9 — Gubernatorial veto. Low probability, high consequence.
**Mechanism.** Only matters if the bill passes. Override is two-thirds of each house (**Art. VII § 8 — subsection UNVERIFIED**). A signaled veto causes leadership to whip against the bill in advance to avoid the confrontation.
**Countermeasure.** Engage the Governor's office during interim, on the lead bill only, framed as taxpayer protection and constitutional fidelity. A bill drawing 2/3 anyway is veto-proof by construction — and note that a bill built to clear 2/3 is exactly the bill that can later carry the joint resolution.

### Rank 10 — Quiet death versus a hearing. Not a failure mode; a strategic choice.
**Mechanism.** A bill that is never agendaed produces nothing: no transcript, no vote, no record, no press, and no way to count who is with you. A bill that gets a hearing and loses 4–3 produces a roll call, a video record, media coverage, and a list of three allies and four targets.
**Countermeasure.** **For this project, a hearing is worth more than a quiet survival.** The author's asset is the moral force of the argument delivered in public by the citizens it is about. Design the first session around *getting a hearing*, not around passing. Fill the room. Then run the same bill in the next session with the record already built. Reserve quiet withdrawal for bills you would rather refile clean.

**Sequenced plan implied by the above:**

| Window | Action |
|---|---|

| Nov 2026–Jan 2027 | Open the bill file. Pre-brief LFA and OLRGC. Lock the sponsor and at least one floor co-sponsor per chamber. |

# THE UTAH GOVERNMENT ACCOUNTABILITY ACT
### Operation Checkmate · a citizen-drafted bill for the 2027 General Session

**THE PROBLEM.** When a Utah citizen breaks the law, the law reaches them in days. When a government actor breaks the law against a citizen, four judge-made immunity doctrines — qualified, judicial, prosecutorial, and sovereign — usually mean nobody is reached at all. The same act, the same harm, two different rules, decided by which of the two people was carrying a badge, a robe, or a title.

**WHAT THE BILL DOES.**
- **Ends state-law immunity as a defense to unlawful conduct.** Immunity protects lawful governance. It does not convert an unlawful act into a lawful one.
- **Makes accountability personal.** The individual who did it answers for it — not only the city, and not only the taxpayer.
- **Bars taxpayer indemnification** of officials found liable for unlawful acts, so misconduct is paid for by the person who committed it instead of out of the public budget.
- **Requires articulable lawful authority.** A government actor exercising power over a citizen must be able to state the authority for it, and the citizen may ask.
- **Adds forfeiture of office and of state-conferred benefits** on a finding of liability, so an official who abuses public power does not keep the power.

**THE UTAH GROUND IT STANDS ON.** This is not imported. Utah's own constitution says all political power is inherent in the people and they have the right to alter or reform their government (art. I, § 2); that all courts shall be open and every person injured shall have remedy by due course of law (art. I, § 11); and that frequent recurrence to fundamental principles is essential to the perpetuity of free government (art. I, § 27). In *Spackman ex rel. Spackman v. Board of Education*, 2000 UT 87, the Utah Supreme Court held that a damages remedy can arise **directly under the Utah Constitution** — Utah already accepts the premise this bill builds on. Utah has also legislated first-in-the-nation before, from asset-forfeiture reform to electronic-data privacy.

**IT HAS BEEN DONE.** Colorado (SB 20-217, 2020) and New Mexico (Civil Rights Act, 2021) both created state causes of action in which qualified immunity is not a defense. Neither state's policing collapsed. Utah can do this better, and from a limited-government premise rather than a borrowed one.

## 3.5 THE OBJECTION-HANDLING TABLE

|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"This is anti-police."** | "It isn't, and the text shows it. The bill reaches judges, prosecutors, regulators, clerks, licensing boards, and school officials — police are one category out of many. And it only reaches *unlawful* conduct. An officer who follows the law is untouched by every line of it. The officers this bill is hard on are the ones the good officers already have to work alongside and answer for. Ask any honest sergeant who he'd rather lose: qualified immunity, or the guy in his squad everybody knows about." |
| 2 | **"The fiscal note is enormous."** | "Look at what the note is measuring. Utah taxpayers already pay for misconduct — through settlements, judgments, insurance premiums, and indemnification. The fiscal question isn't whether misconduct gets paid for. It's who pays. Right now it's your constituents." |
| 3 | **"Cities can't afford this."** | "Cities can't afford the status quo either — ask any city attorney what they carry in liability reserve. This bill reduces municipal exposure, because liability attaches to the person, not only the entity, and cities are barred from indemnifying unlawful conduct. ## 3.6 "WHO ARE YOU AND WHY SHOULD I LISTEN TO YOU?"

This is the question that decides the meeting, and almost every citizen advocate handles it badly — either by inflating ("I've studied this for years, I know more about immunity than most attorneys") or by collapsing ("I'm nobody, I know I'm not qualified"). Both lose the room. Inflation invites a credential contest you will lose. Collapse hands them the exit.

**The move is to answer the question they actually asked.** They are not asking for your résumé. They are asking: *is this person a crank, and will associating with them cost me?* Answer that.

### The scripted answer

> "Fair question, and the honest answer is that I'm nobody in particular. I'm a citizen in [city]. I'm not a lawyer, I've never lobbied, and nobody is paying me.
>
> So don't take my word for anything. That's actually why the whole thing is public on GitHub — every version, every revision, timestamped, open for anyone to attack. And it's why every doctrinal claim in it is tied to a case by name. *Imbler*. *Stump*. *Mireles*. *Harlow*. *Jessop*. You don't have to trust me about any of it. You can read the cases, or hand it to counsel and have them read the cases.
>
> Here's what I'd say I do bring. I read the case law until I understood it, and then I did the work instead of writing a complaint. Most people who feel what I feel about this write an angry email. I wrote a bill. Whatever else is true, that's the thing in front of you — a drafted proposal you can mark up, not a grievance.
>
> And I'll tell you the practical reason a citizen draft is worth your time. I don't have a client. There's no association behind me protecting a carve-out, and nothing in here got softened because somebody's members wouldn't like it. When you get a bill from a lobbyist you know exactly which parts were negotiated and why. This one wasn't negotiated with anyone. That makes it harder to pass and easier to trust.
>
> If you tell me a provision is wrong, I'll change it. I've changed a lot of it already. I'm not attached to my drafting. I'm attached to the principle that the law should apply to the people who enforce it."

### Why this works, and the rules behind it

1. **Concede the credential gap immediately and completely.** It is the true fact in the room. Conceding it first removes it as a weapon — it can only be used against you if you are defending it.
2. **Convert "trust me" into "verify me."** The GitHub repository and the named cases are the actual answer. You are not asking for authority; you are pointing at sources that have their own. This is the single strongest structural move available to a citizen advocate and the author already built it — the repo is an asset, use it in this exact spot.
3. **Name the one asset a citizen genuinely has that no lobbyist has: no client.** This is not a consolation prize. It is a real and rare form of credibility in a legislature where every other document handed over that day was negotiated by someone with a stake. Say it plainly and do not oversell it.
4. **"I wrote a bill instead of an angry email"** is the line that separates you from a crank in one sentence. Cranks do not produce markup-ready drafts. The artifact *is* the credential.
5. **End on amendability.** The fear behind the question is that you are an ideologue who will embarrass them. "Tell me it's wrong and I'll change it — I've changed a lot already" is disconfirming evidence, and it is true.
6. **Never say the word "sovereign," and never litigate your own case history in the room.** If you have a personal story of being harmed by government, it is powerful — but it belongs *after* they have accepted the argument, never as the reason for it. A personal grievance offered as the foundation converts a structural argument into a private one, and private ones do not get carried.
7. **Do not mention the 130,000 words unprompted.** If asked, answer objection #9.
