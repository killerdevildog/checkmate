# For Public Servants — Why Officers, Judges, Prosecutors, and Elected Officials Should Want This Bill

> Operation Checkmate is not a threat. It is a release valve. **Lawful accountability today prevents lawless reactions tomorrow.**

If you are a police officer, a sheriff's deputy, a federal agent, a judge, a prosecutor, a city council member, a mayor, a state legislator, a U.S. Senator or Representative, an agency head, a school official, or any other person clothed with the authority of the state — this guide is written for you.

You may have read the bill, or read one of the community guides ([LGBTQ+](lgbtq.md), [Black community](blacklivesmatter.md), [working class](financial_hardship.md), [conservatives/libertarians/patriots](righists.md)) and concluded that **Operation Checkmate is something to be opposed**. That it adds liability to your work. That it removes immunities you have come to rely on. That it gives citizens with no legal training the power to investigate and indict.

It does. **And here is why you should support it anyway** — not despite that, but because of it.

---

## The structural argument

Throughout history, every society that has produced sustained political violence — from the American Revolution forward — has produced it for the same underlying reason: **the citizens reached a point where they believed they had no peaceful, lawful, accessible means to hold their government accountable.**

> *"Those who make peaceful revolution impossible will make violent revolution inevitable."*
> — President John F. Kennedy, March 13, 1962

This is not a threat. It is a structural fact about human societies. When citizens watch officials abuse power for years with no consequence — when prosecutors decline to charge officers, when judges shield each other behind immunity, when agencies extract from the working class with no accountability, when no avenue exists for a citizen to compel attention to misconduct — the pressure does not disappear. It accumulates. And eventually, at the worst possible moment, it finds an outlet.

That outlet is **rarely a measured, well-targeted civic response**. It is usually angry, untargeted, and dangerous to officials and citizens alike. People march on capitols. People storm courthouses. People take action — sometimes violent action — against the wrong officials, in the wrong places, for the wrong reasons. **Innocent people, including public servants who did nothing wrong, get hurt.**

**Operation Checkmate is the structural alternative.** It provides every citizen — without lawyers, without filing fees, without the cooperation of the offices that may be the source of the problem — a **peaceful, lawful, accessible** mechanism to bring misconduct to account. Twelve citizens. A Joint Declaration of Prosecution. A Council Order for documents. A 36-citizen unanimous Logical Consistency Review. A 64-citizen unanimous Conviction Review.

These are paperwork mechanisms. They run through the County Prosecutor's Office, the Office of the Governor, the courts, the Sheriff's Office. They require zero violence to operate. They are explicitly, structurally, and intentionally nonviolent.

**That is the bill's defining feature, and it is what makes the bill in your interest as a public servant.**

## What this bill is NOT

Before going further, it is worth being explicit:

- **This bill is not a militia document.** It contains no call for, justification of, or sympathy for political violence. Multiple sections expressly require lawful, legal mechanisms.
- **This bill is not a "sovereign citizen" doctrine.** Citizens remain fully subject to the law, including this Act.
- **This bill is not anti-government.** Every legitimate function of government — public safety, the courts, regulation, taxation — remains in place. The bill targets *misconduct at the level of recklessness or higher* (per the §2 mens rea framework), not legitimate exercises of authority.
- **This bill is not a manifesto.** It is a draft statute designed to be passed by the Utah legislature through the ordinary legislative process — and ratified by the citizens through ordinary mechanisms (Article XXIII or analogous).
- **This bill does not encourage you to be afraid of your fellow citizens.** It encourages you to be accountable to them through ordinary legal mechanisms. That is the founders' design.

If you are a public servant who does your job lawfully and within the limits of your authority, **this bill protects you.** It protects you from the bad actors in your own profession who give all of you a bad reputation. It protects you from the conditions of social pressure that make every interaction with citizens harder. It protects your institution from the slow erosion of public trust that always precedes social unrest.

## Why each kind of public servant benefits

### Police officers, deputies, and federal agents

The bill targets **misconduct** — reckless, intentional, or malicious conduct that harms a citizen. It does not reach the officer doing their job lawfully. The mens rea framework (§2) explicitly provides that **good-faith mistakes** do not produce criminal liability — only civil liability at the negligence level.

What the bill changes for the lawful officer:

- **The bad actors are removed.** The officer who plants evidence, the one who lies on a warrant, the one who escalates without cause, the one who beats a suspect in custody — these are the officers who, when they are exposed, make the entire profession look corrupt. **The bill clears the field.**
- **Clearer rules to operate under.** When liability attaches to specific conduct (acting outside the law, using unjustified force, failing to disclose evidence to the prosecutor), officers know where the lines are.
- **Protection from politically-motivated investigations.** The §1.18 Judicial Duty to Engage on the Merits and the §1.20 Duty to Apply Mandatory Rules apply equally to investigations *of* officers. A judge who rubber-stamps a politically-motivated indictment of an officer is reachable under the same provisions as one who rubber-stamps a politically-motivated indictment of any citizen.
- **No more fear of unaccountable bad actors in your department.** Internal Affairs, civilian review boards, and federal monitorships have repeatedly failed to address misconduct. The Citizen Council mechanism (§8) reaches what those bodies have not — meaning the bad cop in the next squad car, the one whose behavior endangers you in the field, finally faces consequences.

### Judges and magistrates

You took an oath to administer the law impartially. Most judges keep that oath. The bill targets the ones who don't:

- The judge who **rubber-stamps** prosecutorial requests without independent evaluation (§1.18) — making your serious work look like theirs.
- The judge who **fails to recuse** from cases with conflicts of interest (§1.17) — eroding trust in *every* judge.
- The judge who **engages in ex parte communications** about pending cases (§1.19) — staining the entire bench.
- The judge who **fails to apply mandatory rules** or correct errors when discovered (§1.20) — making appellate work harder for everyone.

A judge who does the job — engages with the merits, recuses when conflicts appear, follows mandatory rules, corrects errors — has nothing to fear from this bill. **Bad judges undermine public confidence in good judges.** This bill helps protect the integrity of the judiciary by making the worst actors finally answerable.

It also protects you in another way: when a high-profile case produces public outrage at a judicial decision, the citizens have a **lawful, peaceful avenue** to seek review (§8.14 Logical Consistency Review, §8.15 Conviction Review, the §8 Citizen Council generally). Without that avenue, the outrage finds other expressions — some of which have, in our recent history, included threats and violence against judges and their families. **You want that release valve.**

### Prosecutors

The bill imposes **affirmative duties** on prosecutors — to disclose adverse evidence to the jury (§1.14), to stop prosecutions you know are not supportable (§1.15 Malicious Prosecution), and to seek reduction or dismissal when evidence collapses (§1.16). These are duties you already feel professionally — *Brady*, the Rules of Professional Conduct, the constitutional protections you swore to uphold.

What the bill changes:

- **Liability becomes personal, not just institutional.** A *Brady* violation that today produces a vacated conviction (and no consequence to you) tomorrow produces personal civil and criminal liability. **For the prosecutor doing the job ethically, this changes nothing.** For the prosecutor who has cut corners, it changes everything.
- **You gain protection from political pressure.** A district attorney who is asked by a politically-connected official to bring (or decline) a charge can now point to the §1.8 Obstruction provisions — the failure-to-prosecute-because-of-affiliation reaches the political actor pressuring the prosecutor, not just the prosecutor.
- **Bad actors in your office are removed.** Every prosecutor's office has the colleague whose conduct is widely known to be questionable. The bill provides the mechanism to address that without internal political fallout — the Citizen Council can investigate, and the office is the first to benefit when the bad actor is removed.

### Mayors, council members, and elected officials

You ran for office because you wanted to serve your community. The bill protects you in two ways:

- **From the slow erosion of public trust.** When officials at any level abuse power, the trust your constituents had in *all* officials erodes. The bill restores accountability — and with it, trust.
- **From the conditions of unrest.** A community that has lawful, accessible accountability is a community that does not boil over. A community without it becomes harder and more dangerous to govern over time.

The bill also **explicitly carves out dissenting members** of collective bodies (§1.1.1) — *"a member who voted against, formally objected to, or publicly dissociated from the violating action on the record shall not be liable under this subsection for that action."* If you vote against a motion you believe is unlawful, and you put that vote on the record, you are protected from the body's collective liability.

### Senators, Representatives, and federal officials

You took an oath to support and defend the Constitution. The bill operationalizes constitutional principles you have spent your career invoking:

- **The Tenth Amendment.** State authority to define state-law offenses, including offenses applicable to officials in the state.
- **Dual sovereignty.** Federal and state governments as separate sovereigns, each accountable to citizens. The bill cites the Supreme Court precedent (*Lanza*, *Abbate*, *Bartkus*, *Heath*, *Gamble*) that has long supported this.
- **Consent of the governed.** Government deriving its just powers from the consent of the citizens it serves.

A federal official who acts within their lawful authority is not reachable under this bill, even when their conduct affects Utah. The bill explicitly preserves **legitimate preemption analysis** (§14.7) — federal authority that genuinely preempts state law continues to control. What the bill abrogates is the *In re Neagle* (1890) doctrine that has shielded federal officers from state criminal prosecution for state-law offenses they commit. That abrogation rests on the dual-sovereignty doctrine — not on rejection of federal authority.

For elected federal officials: this bill is the most concrete answer you can offer constituents who tell you they have lost faith in government accountability. Supporting a state-level peaceful accountability mechanism is a constitutional act, not a radical one.

## Historical perspective: what happens without this kind of mechanism

Without preaching, and without endorsing any particular event, the historical pattern is clear:

- **The American Revolution** began with a series of grievances about taxation, search and seizure, jury rights, and the judicial process — all of which had been pursued through petitions, lawsuits, and political organization for over a decade before armed conflict began. The colonists turned to arms only after concluding that lawful avenues had been exhausted.
- **The civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s** produced peaceful change through extraordinary discipline — but it also produced the urban unrest documented by the **Kerner Commission Report (1968)**, which concluded that the riots had specific structural causes including unaccountable police misconduct and the absence of meaningful citizen recourse.
- **Recent decades** have seen sustained periods of unrest in many American cities following high-profile police killings — unrest that put officers, citizens, and bystanders in danger. Following each, internal investigations, civilian review boards, federal monitorships, and policy changes were attempted — and each time, the public's verdict has been that those mechanisms did not produce real accountability.

The pattern in each case is the same: **citizens who believe they have lawful recourse use it. Citizens who believe they have no lawful recourse find other recourse.**

Public servants who have lived through any of these moments know how dangerous the second outcome is — to citizens, to officials, and to the institutions of government. **Operation Checkmate is, structurally, the alternative.** It addresses the substance of the grievance (officials acting without consequence) through the form of the law (citizen petitions, councils, prosecutions) — and in doing so, removes the conditions under which violence becomes the alternative.

## The honest case for self-interest

If this bill passes:

- Bad actors in your profession face consequences. **You no longer have to defend their conduct to your community, your family, or yourself.**
- Public trust in your institution can begin to be restored. **Your work becomes easier, not harder.**
- The structural pressure that has been building for decades against unaccountable government finds a peaceful release. **You are safer, your colleagues are safer, your family is safer.**
- The bad actors get removed by the rule of law — not by anyone storming a capitol, ambushing a judge's family, or attacking an officer's home.
- Citizens across the political spectrum get the same answer: *"There is a lawful mechanism. Use it."* Across every community guide that has been written for this bill — left, right, working class, religious, secular — the message is the same: **this is the peaceful way.**

If this bill does **not** pass — or some equivalent state-level accountability bill — the underlying pressure does not disappear. **It accumulates.** And what it produces, eventually, is exactly what every public servant should want to prevent.

## A note on framing

This bill is **not** an attack on public servants. It is a structural protection — for citizens against bad actors in government, **and** for good public servants against the bad actors who give the whole institution a bad name, **and** for the institutions of government against the slow loss of public trust that always precedes social unrest.

It is, in the most direct sense possible, a peace bill. **It exists so that no one ever has to consider less peaceful alternatives.** If you support that — at any level of government, in any role — this bill is in your interest.

The bill targets official **misconduct** at the level of recklessness or higher (per the §2 mens rea framework). Officials who do their jobs lawfully, with reasonable care, in good faith, have nothing to fear. **The bill removes the bad actors so that the good ones can be trusted again.**
