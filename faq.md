## 1. Quick orientation

### What is Operation Checkmate, in one paragraph?

When someone in government — a judge, a cop, a prosecutor, a politician, a school official, a code inspector — uses their power to harm a citizen, **they should face real consequences**. Not just policy reviews. Not just paid leave. Real civil and criminal accountability, the same way you or I would face it if we broke the law. Operation Checkmate closes the gap that currently lets officials act outside the law — sometimes maliciously — without ever being held accountable. And it gives ordinary citizens, not the political class, the means to enforce that accountability.

### Why should I care if I'm not in trouble with the law?

Because almost everyone interacts with government eventually — and when you do, you need to know the people on the other side are accountable. A custody dispute, a traffic stop, a permit, a tax audit, a child-protective-services visit, a school discipline issue, a small-business inspection. The people on the other side of those interactions currently have very limited consequences if they treat you unfairly, dishonestly, or maliciously. Today, the most powerful officials are the ones with the strongest legal shields. This bill flips that: power and accountability go together.

### Why is it called "Checkmate"?

Two reasons. First, the bill is meant to **put every wrongful action of government in check** — restoring the constitutional checks and balances the founders set in place and that have eroded over time. Second, the chess metaphor is unusually exact: in chess, *checkmate* is the position in which the **king** — the highest-ranking piece — has been placed in check and has **no lawful move left** to escape it. The king isn't removed by trickery, assassinated, or overthrown. The king is brought to a stop by the lawful rules of the game, openly and in full view of both players. That is precisely the posture this bill places officials in. They are simply, finally, bound by the same rules that already bind every other piece on the board.

### Who wrote this?

A private citizen in Utah, with help from members of the public who have filed issues and pull requests on the GitHub repository. There is no PAC, no consultancy, no donor class behind it. The bill is licensed for open citizen development — anyone can read it, criticize it, propose changes, fork it for use in another state, or distribute it.

### Is this a real bill?

It is a real, fully drafted statutory proposal — not a press release, not a manifesto, not talking points. The current text is what would be introduced to the Utah Legislature in substantially this form. It has not yet been formally introduced; that requires a sponsoring legislator. Citizen support is part of how that happens.

### How long is it?

The full bill text is several hundred pages in PDF form. The README is a short orientation. The advanced README is a section-by-section walkthrough. This FAQ is the long-form question-and-answer companion. The repository contains community-specific guides under `culture/` for several audiences. You do not need to read all of it — most citizens get the picture from the README plus one community guide.

### What's the easiest way to read it?

Download the latest PDF from the [Releases page](https://github.com/killerdevildog/checkmate/releases/latest) and upload it to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, or Gemini. The PDF is well under 1 MB and fits inside the upload limit of every major AI chat. Then ask the AI any question you want about the bill, in plain language, as if you were talking to a knowledgeable friend.

## 2. Who this affects — the scope of "government actor"

### Who counts as a "government actor" under this bill?

Anyone exercising state power over a citizen — including police officers and sheriff's deputies, judges and magistrates, prosecutors and city attorneys, elected officials at every level, code inspectors, regulators, school officials acting under public authority, child-services workers, agency employees, and any other person clothed with state authority. The bill also reaches collective bodies (city councils, agencies, departments) acting as institutions. The defining test is not job title but **whether the person was exercising delegated public power over a citizen at the moment of the conduct**.

### Does this only apply to police?

No. The bill's framing is deliberately broad because the asymmetry it addresses is not police-specific. Judges, prosecutors, regulators, and elected officials have all built up their own immunity architectures and patterns of abuse. The bill reaches all of them under a single, uniform standard — and through Section 17 names specific role-based offenses for each (police-specific offenses, judicial offenses, prosecutorial offenses, legislative offenses, etc.).

### What about federal officers operating in Utah?

The bill expressly reaches federal officers acting within the State of Utah, and abrogates the *In re Neagle* doctrine that has historically shielded federal officers from state criminal prosecution for conduct taken within the State. The reasoning: a state has the sovereign authority to defend its citizens from any government actor who commits a crime against them on Utah soil — federal status is not a license to commit state-defined offenses. This provision is one of the most pointed in the bill and is a major focus of the conservative / federalism community guide ([culture/righists.md](culture/righists.md)).

### What about private contractors doing government work?

The bill's "color of authority" and "clothed with state power" definitions reach private contractors performing government functions — including private prison and detention staff, private probation officers, private debt-collection contractors enforcing court-ordered debt, private vendors of government technology that exercises decisional authority over citizens, and similar actors. The principle: if you are exercising the state's power over a citizen, you are bound by the state's rules.

### Does this apply to off-duty police, judges, or other officials?

Two principles apply in parallel. **§5.3 (Official-Capacity Rights Gap)** says that an actor exercising official capacity cannot invoke personal rights as a shield against accountability — the badge constrains, not expands, the actor's rights. **§5.4 (Private-Capacity Jurisdiction Gap)** says that an actor's claim to be off-duty does not shield them from Citizen Council jurisdiction or generally applicable criminal law for private-capacity conduct. An official who commits a crime as a private citizen faces the ordinary criminal code. An official acting officially faces the heightened public-servant standard. The actor cannot use official capacity to expand rights or private capacity to escape accountability.

### Are jurors government actors?

Yes, when serving on a jury. The bill recognizes jurors as exercising state power during the trial process and applies modified accountability standards to them — including the requirement that convicting jurors record their reasoning on each piece of material evidence, so that the truly innocent can appeal on the facts.

### Does this reach school officials, school resource officers, and school discipline?

Yes, where the conduct involves the exercise of state authority — investigations, discipline that triggers law enforcement, school-to-prison-pipeline patterns, abusive truancy enforcement, and the use of school resource officers as criminal-process enforcement against ordinary childhood conduct. Section 17.9N (Juvenile Authority Abuse) specifically addresses misuse of school discipline, truancy enforcement, juvenile probation, and placement authority.

### Does the bill apply retroactively?

The bill includes a **Retroactive Civil Applicability** section (Section 10) that allows civil reach for certain past conduct within a defined window. Criminal liability is generally prospective only, consistent with constitutional ex post facto principles. The civil retroactive reach is calibrated narrowly to allow citizens to recover for past harm without raising ex post facto problems on the criminal side.

## 3. The immunity question

### What is qualified immunity, and how does the bill end it?

Qualified immunity is a Supreme Court-created doctrine (*Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800) that shields government officials from civil liability for constitutional violations unless the right at issue was **"clearly established at the time of the conduct"** — meaning, in practice, that no previous court had specifically held the same factual conduct to be unlawful. This has produced absurd results — officers shielded for stealing during a search because no prior court had specifically held that officers stealing during a search violated clearly established law (*Jessop v. City of Fresno*, 936 F.3d 937). The bill abrogates qualified immunity within Utah for any conduct meeting the elements of any offense defined in the Act. The "clearly established law" inquiry has no application. The standard is the substantive elements of the offense — recklessness, intent, or malice — not whether some prior court has ruled on identical facts.

### What is judicial immunity, and why does the bill end it?

Judicial immunity is the doctrine that a judge cannot be sued for judicial acts within their jurisdiction even where the conduct is malicious, corrupt, or grievously injurious. The foundational case (*Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. 335) established that this immunity is **not lost** by allegations that the act was done maliciously or corruptly. Subsequent cases extended it to *Stump v. Sparkman* (a judge approving the surgical sterilization of a 15-year-old girl without a hearing) and *Mireles v. Waco* (a judge ordering police to attack a public defender with excessive force). The bill abrogates this within Utah: a judge who commits a violation under the Act is personally liable, civilly and criminally, regardless of whether the conduct was "judicial in nature" or "within jurisdiction."

### What is prosecutorial immunity?

Prosecutorial immunity is the absolute civil immunity prosecutors enjoy for acts in their "advocate" role, even where the prosecutor knowingly used false testimony (*Imbler v. Pachtman*, 424 U.S. 409). Subsequent cases extended this to selective-prosecution decisions, charging decisions, and grand-jury presentations. The bill abrogates prosecutorial immunity within Utah. A prosecutor who commits a violation under the Act — including selective non-prosecution, fabrication or knowing use of false evidence, or obstruction of a Citizen Council — is personally liable, civilly and criminally.

### What is sovereign immunity, and does the bill waive it?

Sovereign immunity is the doctrine that the State and its agencies cannot be sued without consent. By adopting this Act, the State of Utah **expressly waives sovereign immunity** for any claim arising under this title. The waiver is part of the Act itself, not subject to separate consent in each case. Sovereign and governmental immunity cannot bar civil suit against the State, its political subdivisions, its agencies, or any government actor under this title.

### Doesn't qualified immunity exist to protect "good cops" from frivolous lawsuits?

That's the policy argument that's used to defend it. The empirical record shows the opposite: qualified immunity routinely shields officers who have done things that any private citizen would face prosecution for — and the cases get dismissed at summary judgment without the underlying conduct ever being adjudicated on the merits. Honest officers acting within the law have nothing to fear from the bill's mens rea requirements (recklessness, intent, or malice). What gets stripped is the shield for the conduct that should never have been protected.

### What about good-faith mistakes?

The bill recognizes four levels of mental state on both the criminal and civil tracks: **negligent**, **reckless**, **intentional**, and **malicious**. Recklessness is the default criminal floor unless a specific section says otherwise. Negligent conduct can still be criminal where the bill expressly classifies it that way (certain infractions, failure-to-serve rules, or specifically defined negligent official conduct), but the general posture is that honest mistakes within lawful authority that do not meet a criminally enforceable mens rea are not the target. Good-faith adherence to the law remains protected.

### What if a federal court strikes down the immunity-abrogation provisions?

The bill is severable — if any one provision is held unconstitutional, the remainder continues in force. The immunity provisions are written to track state-law sovereign-power principles and the well-established proposition that immunity doctrines are common-law constructs that legislatures can modify or eliminate. The bill also includes express anti-circumvention language in Section 16 to prevent the same conduct being shielded through different doctrines.

### Why criminal liability — isn't civil liability enough?

Civil liability has been tried for fifty years through 42 U.S.C. § 1983 and its analogues, and it has been hollowed out by qualified immunity, indemnification by the employing agency, and the practical reality that even successful civil suits do not change institutional behavior. Criminal liability is different. It attaches personally to the actor; it cannot be indemnified away by the employer; it carries the same career, reputational, and freedom consequences that any other defendant faces; and — uniquely — it imposes the same kind of discipline on the third regime of state power (civilian government) that the criminal code already imposes on the first (citizens) and that the UCMJ imposes on the second (military). Without the criminal side, the asymmetry remains.

## 4. Citizen Councils — the enforcement mechanism

### What is a Citizen Council?

A Citizen Council is a body of twelve or more citizens, self-organized under the procedures of Section 9, that has the power to investigate official misconduct, convene witnesses, issue findings, and effectively indict where the elements of an offense under this Act are met. Councils are how the bill ensures that the prosecutors and judges whose conduct the bill reaches cannot be the same people deciding whether the bill is ever enforced.

### Why a Citizen Council instead of just trusting prosecutors?

Because the prosecutors are part of the regime the bill reaches. A prosecutor cannot be expected to charge another prosecutor for selective non-prosecution. A prosecutor cannot be expected to charge a judge who is reviewing the prosecutor's other cases. A prosecutor cannot be expected to charge an officer who is the prosecutor's primary investigative resource. The Citizen Council exists because the gatekeeper problem is structural — and the only way to fix it is to put accountability in the hands of people who are not part of the regime being held to account.

### How is a Citizen Council formed?

Under Section 9, a group of twelve or more eligible citizens may convene themselves as a Citizen Council with respect to a specific matter or pattern of conduct. The Council follows the formation and operational procedures in the section. No government permission, license, or pre-authorization is required to form a Council — the right to convene is part of the Act itself.

### Who can serve on a Citizen Council?

Eligible citizens — generally, adult citizens of Utah who are not current government actors with respect to the matter under investigation and who are not within the immediate family of the subject of the investigation. The bill includes conflict-of-interest provisions to prevent the Council mechanism from being weaponized for personal vendettas.

### What can a Citizen Council actually do?

A Council can investigate, gather evidence, compel testimony under the procedures of Section 9, issue findings, and effectively indict where the elements of an offense under this Act are met. The Council's findings, in conjunction with the bill's mechanisms for activating prosecution, give citizens a direct route to accountability that does not depend on the cooperation of the prosecutor whose conduct may be at issue.

### What stops Councils from being weaponized to harass innocent officials?

Several built-in safeguards: a quorum requirement of at least twelve citizens (no single complainant); conflict-of-interest rules; the requirement that any finding meet the substantive elements of an actual offense under the Act (mens rea, conduct, harm); the same evidentiary standards that apply in any other Utah proceeding; criminal liability for knowingly false complaints under generally applicable Utah perjury and false-reporting statutes; and the fact that any subsequent judicial proceeding still requires the ordinary safeguards of due process for the accused.

### How is a Citizen Council different from a grand jury?

A grand jury is summoned by the government, instructed by a prosecutor, and operates under the prosecutor's effective control. A Citizen Council is convened by citizens, operates under the procedures of the Act, and is structurally independent of the prosecutor. The Council exists precisely because the grand jury has, in practice, been captured by prosecutorial control — a phenomenon that the saying *"a grand jury would indict a ham sandwich if the prosecutor asked"* captures from one direction, and that the failure of grand juries to indict officers in clearly meritorious cases captures from the other.

### Will Citizen Councils overload the courts?

The bill includes meaningful safeguards: charges require a defined mental state, Citizen Councils require twelve or more citizens to act in concert, and limited declination grounds are preserved. Frivolous complaints get filtered out the same way they do today. What changes is that **legitimate** complaints can no longer be filtered out by the very people they target. The expected effect is not a flood of meritless cases but a meaningful subset of meritorious cases that currently never make it to court at all.

### Can I form a Citizen Council myself?

You can start the process — but a Council requires twelve or more eligible citizens to convene. The right to organize one is part of the Act; the procedural requirements (quorum, conflict checks, procedures) are spelled out in Section 9. Citizens who want to engage with this mechanism are encouraged to do so jointly, not unilaterally.

## 5. What triggers liability — mens rea, due process, and substance

### What are the four mens rea levels in the bill?

The bill recognizes four levels on both the criminal and civil tracks: **negligent**, **reckless**, **intentional**, and **malicious**. Each level corresponds to a different culpability threshold and a different liability tier. Recklessness is the default criminal floor unless a specific section says otherwise.

### What does each mens rea level mean in practice?

- **Negligent** — failed to exercise the care that a reasonable government actor in the position would exercise. Generally civil, except where the bill expressly classifies a negligent act as criminal.
- **Reckless** — knew or should have known of a substantial risk and proceeded anyway. The default criminal threshold.
- **Intentional** — meant to do the conduct, with awareness of its substantial certainty to cause the harm.
- **Malicious** — intentional conduct accompanied by ill will, evil motive, retaliatory purpose, desire to punish, desire to intimidate, desire to conceal wrongdoing, or desire to harm a citizen. Malicious conduct triggers maximum aggravation and is reachable as the highest tier wherever malice is shown.

### What does "color of authority" mean?

"Color of authority" or "color of law" describes conduct that an actor takes under the apparent backing of public power — even if the actor exceeds their actual lawful authority in doing so. An officer who claims authority to search without a warrant is acting under color of law even though the search itself is unlawful. The bill explicitly addresses color-of-authority conduct because much of the worst official misconduct is committed precisely by exceeding actual authority while invoking the appearance of it.

### What does "ultra vires" mean?

*Ultra vires* is a Latin phrase meaning "beyond the powers" — conduct outside the lawful scope of the actor's office, role, contract, appointment, commission, delegation, or other source of authority. The bill specifies three categories of ultra vires conduct: the actor takes an action no law authorizes; the actor takes an action only some other officer is authorized to take; or the actor performs a function constitutionally assigned to a different branch of government (executive performing a legislative function, judicial actor performing a legislative function, legislative actor performing a judicial function, etc.). Ultra vires conduct is per se without lawful authority.

### What is "Anarchy" in the bill's framework?

Under §1.1.10, *anarchy* (in the government context) means a government actor exercising state power **completely outside any identifiable lawful authority**. An officer who stops a citizen without articulable basis, a judge who issues an order with no statutory or constitutional foundation, an agency that enforces a rule it was never authorized to make — these are reachable as Anarchy. The name is deliberately provocative because the principle is too: government conduct without lawful authority is, by definition, lawless.

### What is "Cabalism" in the bill's framework?

*Cabalism* (under §1.1.6) is the coordinated action of two or more government actors to harm a citizen, retaliate against a citizen, conceal wrongdoing, or extract benefit through misuse of public power. It captures the pattern of small groups of officials acting together — the city council member, the police chief, the prosecutor, and the judge who all happen to coordinate against a single citizen or family. Where the elements are met, cabalism is reachable as a serious felony.

### What does "battery against rights" mean under the bill?

The bill's §1.1.3 redefines battery in the government context to include the unlawful violation of a citizen's constitutional or statutory rights through the unauthorized use of state power. This recognizes that government can harm a citizen not only by direct physical contact but by violating rights — through unlawful search, unlawful seizure, unlawful detention, unlawful family separation, unlawful retaliation, and similar conduct. The result is that rights violations under color of authority are reachable as a recognized criminal offense, not merely as a tort.

### What is "Obstruction of Justice" in the bill's framework, and how does it cover prosecutorial declination?

Obstruction of Justice (§1.1.8) reaches conduct that prevents, delays, impedes, conceals, or distorts the lawful operation of a proceeding, investigation, record, duty, or official function. The provision expressly covers **selective non-prosecution** — a prosecutor who declines to charge their friend, fellow official, or political ally for a real offense is obstructing justice, at the same severity as the underlying offense. This addresses the structural problem that prosecutors do not police themselves or their allies.

### What is "Official Intimidation" under the bill?

Official Intimidation (§1.1.13) reaches conduct by a government actor that places, or that the actor knew would place, a citizen in fear of harm to their person, family, livelihood, liberty, property, or rights — beyond what any legitimate purpose of the office requires. This is the offense that reaches the judge's "if you don't shut up I'll hold you in contempt" threat, the officer's "do this or I'll find something to charge you with" maneuver, the regulator's "comply with my demand or I'll make your life difficult," and similar abuses of official position.

### What is "Terrorism" in the government context?

Under §1.1.12, *terrorism* in the government context reaches conduct by a government actor that uses threat, fear, or coercive harm to a citizen's safety, livelihood, family, business, or rights, on a scale or with a malice sufficient to constitute terror against the citizen. The classification is deliberately serious because the conduct it reaches — sustained terror against an individual citizen, family, or community by people wielding state power — is among the gravest wrongs a government can commit.

### What is the difference between "negligent" official conduct and "incompetence in office"?

Negligent official conduct is a general mens rea category — failing to exercise reasonable care. Incompetence in Office (§1.1.20 and §17.5A) is a distinct offense reaching demonstrated inability to perform the functions of office — including the citizen-prompted oath-and-authority recitation provision, under which an actor who cannot substantially recite the oath, the Supremacy Clause, the controlling constitutional provisions, and the statutory authority being exercised is, by that failure, prima facie incompetent in office.

## 6. For specific groups — business, family, employee, parent, journalist

### I'm a small business owner — how does this protect me?

The bill defines *business* in §1.1.21 and operationalizes business reachability under §5.7. If a government actor or governing body engages in unlawful, ultra vires, retaliatory, coercive, obstructive, or otherwise wrongful conduct that causes your business demonstrable economic injury, the business has standing to sue under §5.7.1. If that injury pushes you below the federal poverty line, causes the loss of your family home, or causes the loss of meaningful transportation — any one of these — the conduct is also reachable as a criminal offense against you as a natural-person beneficiary under §5.7.3. The underlying right reached is your **right to pursue happiness** — to build a livelihood, maintain a household, and seek a decent living through lawful work.

### I'm an employee whose employer was destroyed by a government agency — can I sue?

Yes, under §1.1.22 and §5.7.2. The employee has **independent civil standing** — not derivative of any claim by the employer — where wrongful government conduct against the employer causes the employee to lose income, hours, or position such that the employee or the employee's household falls below the poverty line, or loses meaningful transportation. The employee may bring the claim whether or not the employer pursues, settles, or even continues to exist. The defendant is the government actor, not the employer. At-will status, arbitration clauses, and similar private contract terms cannot defeat the employee's standing.

### I'm a parent. How does this protect family integrity?

Several provisions reach family-targeted misconduct. **§1.1.19 (Family Separation)** defines the unlawful removal, restriction, or termination of family relationships without due process as a recognized form of wrongful conduct. **§17.9I (Poverty-Based Family Separation)** specifically reaches child-welfare actions taken primarily on the basis of poverty, disability, housing instability, lack of transportation, or inability to access services. **§17.9J (Family-Rights Advisement Failure)** addresses the failure to advise parents of their rights in child-welfare proceedings. The bill recognizes parenting as a protected right and treats family separation by a government actor without lawful basis as a serious offense — reachable up through capital classification where elements are met.

### I'm a journalist. Does this protect press freedom?

The bill protects journalists in several ways. §1.1.5 expressly includes free press and expression among protected rights. §17.2 (Institutional Retaliation) reaches retaliation against citizens for their status as journalists or critics. §2 due-process provisions and the recording-related sections protect the right to record government officials in the performance of their public duties. And the Hearsay and Statement Evidence provisions of §2.3 specifically address the admissibility of statement evidence so that journalists' contemporaneous reporting can be used to establish facts in proceedings under the Act.

### I'm a whistleblower. What does the bill do for me?

Whistleblower protection runs throughout the Act. §17.2 (Institutional Retaliation) reaches retaliation motivated by the citizen's status as a whistleblower, complainant, or witness. §1.1.18 (Retaliatory) defines retaliatory conduct broadly. §17.2A (Process-Stacking Retaliation) reaches the pattern of using multiple official processes (charges, investigations, inspections, audits) in sequence to wear down a whistleblower. Where retaliation affects housing, utilities, business license, employment, child welfare, court access, or election participation, the offense rises to capital classification.

### I'm homeless. Does this address how I get treated by police?

Yes. §17.7B (Status-Based Survival Enforcement) reaches government action that cites, arrests, detains, removes property from, or otherwise punishes a citizen for unavoidable life-sustaining conduct arising from homelessness, poverty, disability, illness, lack of shelter, lack of transportation, or other survival necessity. The provision expressly rejects "sanitation, aesthetics, nuisance, public discomfort, tourism, business impact, or quality of life" as defenses where the enforcement targets survival conduct and no realistic lawful alternative existed.

### I'm a public defender. Does this protect my ability to do my job?

Yes. §2.11 specifically addresses public defenders — including the right to refuse an overwhelming caseload that prevents effective representation, parity-of-arms provisions ensuring public defenders have access to investigative and expert resources comparable to the prosecution, and protection against retaliation for vigorous defense. The bill recognizes that ineffective assistance of counsel (§1.1.17) is often a symptom of systemic underfunding and overload rather than individual lawyer failure.

### I'm an immigrant or a non-citizen. Does the bill protect me?

Yes. The bill's protections run to "citizens" in the broadly inclusive sense the constitutional tradition has used — meaning persons subject to the jurisdiction of the State of Utah. The bill does not condition its protections on immigration status, and several provisions explicitly reach immigration-related abuses of process (family separation through immigration proceedings, process-stacking retaliation based on immigration status, etc.). The Fourteenth Amendment principle that the Constitution protects "persons" not only "citizens" is preserved.

### I'm a juror or a witness. Am I protected?

Yes. Jurors are recognized as exercising state power and are subject to the modified accountability standards of the Act, but they are also protected against retaliation and intimidation. Witnesses are protected under §17.2 (Institutional Retaliation) and §1.1.13 (Official Intimidation). The bill recognizes that the integrity of the justice system depends on jurors and witnesses being able to participate without fear.

### I work in government and I support this bill. What do I do?

Read [`culture/public_servants.md`](culture/public_servants.md), which is the community guide written specifically for officials. The short version: this bill protects honest officials by removing the bad actors who give every profession a bad name. Quiet internal support, conversations with colleagues, and — if you are an elected official — sponsorship or co-sponsorship are all paths forward. The bill protects dissenting council members who object on the record.

## 7. Constitutional questions and federalism

### Is this constitutional?

Yes — and the bill is drafted with constitutional architecture in mind. The bill operates entirely within Utah's sovereign authority to define state crimes, regulate state officers, waive its own sovereign immunity, and modify common-law doctrines. The immunity doctrines the bill abrogates are common-law constructs (or, in the case of qualified immunity, judicially created constructions of § 1983) that legislatures have unquestioned authority to modify or eliminate within their own jurisdictions.

### What about the Supremacy Clause? Doesn't federal law preempt this?

Federal law preempts state law in narrowly defined circumstances — where Congress has occupied the field, where there is direct conflict, or where state law obstructs federal purposes. None of those circumstances applies to a state law that imposes additional accountability on state officials for state offenses. Federal officers acting within Utah are reachable under the bill on principles of state sovereign authority that predate and survive the Supremacy Clause — a point §14.7 of the bill addresses directly with respect to the *In re Neagle* doctrine.

### What about the Fourteenth Amendment?

The bill explicitly invokes Fourteenth Amendment protections (due process, equal protection, privileges and immunities) and applies them through state law. The bill does not weaken any federal constitutional protection — it strengthens accountability for violations of those protections within Utah.

### What if a federal court strikes down part of the bill?

The bill is severable. If any one provision is held unconstitutional, the remainder continues in force. The bill also includes a Section 16 anti-circumvention provision that addresses attempts to use federal-court decisions to shield conduct that the bill independently reaches.

### Why state law instead of pushing for federal reform?

Federal reform of qualified immunity has been blocked for decades by the political economy of police unions, prosecutor associations, and the institutional resistance of federal judges who themselves benefit from the immunity architecture. State-level action does not require any of those gatekeepers. A single state — Utah — can demonstrate that the model works, and other states can adopt the same framework. The bill is written from the outset as a model bill, intended to be portable to every state.

### Doesn't this violate separation of powers within Utah?

No. The bill is a substantive criminal and civil statute — a quintessential exercise of the legislative power. It does not direct courts how to decide individual cases. It does not displace executive enforcement discretion at the prosecutorial level except where that discretion has been used to obstruct justice (which is itself a criminal offense). The Citizen Council mechanism is an exercise of the inherent citizen power that the constitutional architecture has always presupposed.

### What about the Utah Constitution?

The bill is drafted to be consistent with the Utah Constitution and to give effect to several of its provisions — Article I (Declaration of Rights), the natural-rights language, and the provisions on the rights of the accused — that have been underutilized in Utah jurisprudence. Where the bill goes further than the Utah Constitution requires, it does so as a permissible exercise of legislative power to provide additional protection to citizens.

### Is this a "sovereign citizen" thing?

**No. Emphatically not. This Act is the opposite of sovereign-citizen ideology and explicitly rejects every premise of that movement.**

The "sovereign citizen" movement is a loose collection of fringe groups holding the belief that ordinary citizens are not subject to most or all government law — that they can opt out of taxation, licensing, traffic laws, and court jurisdiction through pseudo-legal theories (UCC filings, "strawman" doctrine claiming a person and the all-capital-letters version of their name are separate legal entities, special name-formatting rules, the "redemption" theory that claims the federal government holds secret accounts in each citizen's name, "Affidavits of Truth," and various paperwork they claim overrides ordinary law and court orders). The FBI has classified some segments of the movement as a domestic-terrorism concern because of recurring violent confrontations with law enforcement during encounters in which adherents refused to recognize the authority of officers, judges, or courts. **Sovereign-citizen ideology is, in plain terms, anti-law.**

Operation Checkmate is **pro-law**. Citizens remain fully subject to the law, including this Act. The Act's premise is that the law applies to *everyone* — including officials currently exempted by the immunity architecture — and the entire architecture this Act creates operates within the constitutional order. Citizen Councils convene under defined statutory procedures (Section 9). Charges follow defined mens rea standards (Section 3 — negligent, reckless, intentional, malicious). Prosecutions follow ordinary due-process protections. Appeals follow ordinary appellate procedure. Nothing in this Act authorizes citizens to refuse lawful taxes, ignore traffic laws, refuse to identify themselves to officers, disregard lawful court orders, or take any extra-legal action of any kind. The Act explicitly disavows violence, militia framings, "constitutional sheriff" narratives, and extra-constitutional conduct in its own text.

It is true, as a matter of bedrock American constitutional law dating back to *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), that **a statute, ordinance, regulation, or order that is genuinely unconstitutional is not, in the strict legal sense, enforceable**. This Act addresses that through **constitutional means** — Section 16 (Nullity of Government Instruments Repugnant to This Act or to Constitutional Authority) declares repugnant instruments null through orderly judicial process, and gives citizens a defined statutory pathway to challenge them. That is the critical distinction between this Act and sovereign-citizen ideology. **The Act addresses unconstitutional government conduct *through* the constitutional order — legislation, judicial review, and citizen mechanisms grounded in statute. Sovereign-citizen ideology, by contrast, claims the right to *unilaterally declare* which laws apply to the individual, with no orderly process and no recognition of the constitutional order itself. Those two postures are not adjacent. They are opposites.**

**Any framing of Operation Checkmate as a sovereign-citizen, militia, "constitutional sheriff," or anti-government project misrepresents it.** This Act exists to *strengthen* the rule of law, not to weaken it; to *restore* the constitutional checks the founders built, not to abolish them; to bind *everyone* equally to the law, not to exempt anyone — citizen or official — from it. Any individual or organization attempting to associate this bill with sovereign-citizen ideology is either mistaken about what the bill says or actively misrepresenting it. The text of the bill, the explanatory documents in this repository, and the public communications around the project all uniformly reject every premise sovereign-citizen ideology rests on.

## 8. How a citizen actually engages the bill

### How do I file a complaint under the bill?

If the bill is adopted, the procedures of Section 9 control. Complaints can be brought through several channels: directly to the County Prosecutor's Office, through the Sheriff's Office, through a Citizen Council that has convened on the matter, or through any other channel the bill makes available. No filing fee is required. No lawyer is required (though one is recommended for any significant matter).

### Do I need a lawyer?

No — but a lawyer is recommended for any significant matter. The bill is explicitly drafted so that an ordinary citizen, without legal training, can read it, understand it, and engage with its mechanisms. The Citizen Council process is designed for citizen participation. Civil suits under the bill follow ordinary Utah civil procedure, and pro se representation is permitted.

### What if the prosecutor refuses to charge?

That is the precise scenario the bill addresses. Prosecutorial refusal to charge a legitimate complaint is potentially obstruction of justice under §1.1.8 (Obstruction — Selective Non-Prosecution). The Citizen Council mechanism of Section 9 exists in part to provide an alternative path when prosecutors refuse to act. The bill's activation mechanisms allow citizens to move forward even when the prosecutor will not.

### What if the police investigate themselves and clear themselves?

Self-investigation is a recognized pattern of obstruction under the bill. Section 9.10 specifically addresses Council jurisdiction over private-capacity conduct by government actors, and the Council mechanism is intended to operate independently of internal affairs processes. A police-clears-police outcome does not bar a Council from independently investigating, and findings adverse to the cleared officer are admissible under the ordinary evidentiary rules.

### What if the judge throws out my case?

The bill includes provisions addressing judicial obstruction. A judge who dismisses a case under this Act without lawful basis — to protect a fellow official, to suppress a meritorious claim, or to obstruct the operation of the Act — is themselves reachable as a government actor under this Act. Appellate review and Council recourse remain available.

### What's the statute of limitations?

The bill includes calibrated limitations periods that take into account the difficulty of bringing claims against officials who control the evidence and the process. Limitations are tolled during the period in which the official actively obstructs discovery or proceedings. Retroactive civil applicability under Section 10 reaches certain past conduct within a defined window.

### Can convictions under this Act be expunged?

Convictions of government actors under this Act follow the ordinary Utah rules on expungement, with one exception: convictions involving the wrongful conduct categories of §1.1.19 (theft under color of authority, fabrication, retaliation, family separation without lawful basis, etc.) are generally not eligible for expungement, because the offenses themselves involve a breach of public trust whose record is itself a matter of public interest.

### Can I bring a civil claim and a criminal complaint at the same time?

Yes. Civil and criminal tracks operate in parallel, as they do under existing Utah law. The mens rea standards differ (civil is generally a lower threshold), the burdens of proof differ (preponderance for civil, beyond a reasonable doubt for criminal), and the remedies differ (damages vs. criminal penalty). A citizen with both a civil and criminal claim may pursue either or both.

### What does the petition do?

It demonstrates citizen support. The more first names and zip codes the bill receives, the harder it is for elected officials in Utah to ignore the demand for it. **This is the part where you matter most** — the bill exists, the architecture is drafted, and what remains is the political will to introduce, debate, and adopt it. Citizen demand is how that political will is built.

## 9. Penalties, classifications, and consequences

### What classifications does the bill use?

The bill uses the standard Utah classification scheme: infractions, misdemeanors (Class B, Class A, Class 1 / high misdemeanor), and felonies (Class 4, Class 3, Class 2, Class 1), plus capital classification for the most serious offenses. Each offense in the bill is assigned a classification that reflects the severity of the conduct.

### Why does the bill include capital classifications?

Capital classification is reserved for the most serious offenses — those involving government conduct that has caused, or has substantially contributed to, the death of a citizen, the death of a child, the suicide of a victim, the destruction of a family, or comparable irreversible harm. The bill takes the position that conduct of that severity, when committed by a government actor wielding state power, is at least as grave as any other capital offense recognized in the criminal code. The classification is intentional and is not limited to homicide.

### What is "forfeiture of office"?

Section 11 provides that conviction under this Act results in forfeiture of public office, forfeiture of eligibility for any future public office, forfeiture of any government contract held by the actor or any entity in which the actor holds a controlling or beneficial interest, and forfeiture of future eligibility for government contracts, grants, subsidies, licenses, or other awards of public funds. The principle: public power is a trust, and conviction for breach of that trust forfeits the trust.

### What about pensions?

Where the offense involves a serious breach of public trust, the bill provides for forfeiture of the public pension associated with the office in which the misconduct occurred. The principle is consistent with existing Utah and federal practice for serious public-corruption offenses, applied more broadly to the kinds of misconduct the bill reaches.

### Does the bill impose any sanctions on the State or local government — separate from the individual actor?

Yes. Section 11 and related provisions reach entity-level government actors (cities, counties, agencies, departments) as well as individual officers. Where a government body has knowingly tolerated, ratified, or encouraged the underlying misconduct, the body itself can be held accountable through fines, forfeiture of grants, and other entity-level remedies.

### Does the State pay individual judgments against officers?

No. Section 5 expressly forbids using state funds to indemnify the individual government actor for liability under this Act. The actor faces the consequences personally. The principle is essential to deterrence: indemnification would simply transfer the cost to taxpayers and eliminate the personal accountability the bill is designed to create.

### What happens to ongoing cases involving officials convicted under this Act?

The bill includes provisions addressing the integrity of cases in which a convicted official participated — including review of convictions obtained through fabricated evidence, coerced pleas, or other conduct reachable under the Act, and citizen access to records sufficient to challenge tainted convictions.

## 10. Politics and ideology — is this left, right, or neither

### Is this a left-wing bill?

No — and it isn't a right-wing bill either. The bill is structured around principles that have support across the political spectrum: equal application of the law, restoration of constitutional checks and balances, citizen accountability over public power, removal of immunity for those who abuse public office, and protection of rights regardless of the rights-holder's political identity. The immunity doctrines the bill abrogates have been criticized by figures across the political spectrum — including by Supreme Court Justices nominated by presidents of both parties.

### Isn't this anti-cop / anti-judge / anti-government?

No. The bill leaves every legitimate function of government untouched. It only reaches **misconduct** — and only at the level of recklessness or higher (unless a specific section makes negligent official conduct, failure to serve, or an infraction criminally enforceable). Officials who do their job lawfully and in good faith have nothing to fear from this bill. Bad actors in any profession make the whole profession look corrupt; this bill clears the field.

### Does the bill take a position on policing reform debates (defund, abolish, etc.)?

No. The bill is silent on funding levels, staffing models, organizational restructuring, or any other policy debate about how policing should be organized. Its sole concern is accountability for misconduct, regardless of whether the misconduct occurs in a well-funded department or a poorly-funded one, in a large department or a small one. The bill is compatible with a wide range of substantive policy positions on policing — what it is not compatible with is the continued immunity of officers who commit obvious wrongs.

### Does the bill take a position on judicial reform debates?

No. The bill does not address judicial selection, term lengths, court structure, or other systemic judicial-reform questions. Its concern is the immunity that has shielded individual judges from accountability for individual misconduct. Citizens with strong views on broader judicial reform can hold those views and support this bill; they are independent.

## 11. How this compares to existing remedies

### How is this different from 42 U.S.C. § 1983?

Section 1983 is the principal federal civil rights statute. It allows civil suits against state officials for violations of federal constitutional rights — and has been almost entirely hollowed out by qualified immunity, the "clearly established law" test, indemnification, and the practical barriers to bringing such suits. Operation Checkmate is different in several ways: it operates at the state level (Utah); it includes a criminal track in addition to civil; it abrogates qualified immunity within Utah; it gives citizens (through Councils) a direct enforcement mechanism rather than depending on plaintiffs' lawyers and federal court; and it covers a much broader range of misconduct than constitutional-violation suits alone.

### How is this different from the existing Utah criminal code?

The existing Utah criminal code (Title 76) defines crimes that apply to all persons — including, in theory, government actors. In practice, prosecutors decline to charge government actors for conduct that ordinary citizens would routinely be charged for. The Operation Checkmate bill addresses both halves of the problem: it defines specific offenses for government conduct that has been historically under-prosecuted, and it gives citizens a Council-based mechanism to force action when prosecutors decline. It also abrogates the immunity doctrines that have prevented some criminal liability from attaching even where it nominally exists.

### How is this different from federal civil rights criminal statutes (18 U.S.C. § 242)?

The federal criminal civil rights statute exists but is rarely used — federal prosecutors typically reserve it for the most egregious cases and require evidence of specific intent to deprive constitutional rights. Operation Checkmate operates at the state level, applies a broader mens rea standard, covers a wider range of conduct, and is enforceable through the Citizen Council mechanism — meaning the bill does not depend on federal prosecutorial cooperation to function.

### How is this different from internal affairs / police review boards?

Internal affairs and civilian review boards are administrative bodies operating within or alongside the police agency they review. They have, in practice, been captured by the institutions they are meant to oversee — and they lack the power to impose criminal liability, force prosecution, or compel transparency. Operation Checkmate's Citizen Councils are structurally independent of the bodies they review, have substantive legal powers grounded in statute, and are not subject to the institutional incentives that have hollowed out internal review.

### How is this different from the Department of Justice consent-decree process?

DOJ consent decrees address patterns and practices of misconduct in particular police departments, negotiated between DOJ and the locality, enforced through federal court oversight. They are expensive, slow, episodic, dependent on political will at the federal level, and have a mixed empirical record. Operation Checkmate operates as standing state law — it does not wait for DOJ to act, does not require negotiation with the locality, and applies uniformly to every government actor in the state. It is also structured to reach individual misconduct, not only patterns.

### How is this different from civilian oversight of the military (UCMJ)?

The Uniform Code of Military Justice is the disciplinary code that governs the U.S. military. It is comprehensive, real, and enforced. Operation Checkmate is, in essence, the parallel disciplinary code for the third regime of authority — the civilian government acting on citizens — that has never had one. The UCMJ's existence is part of the moral case for this bill: if it is right that the military be governed by a real disciplinary code, it is right that the civilian government be governed by one too.

## 12. Adoption, adaptation, and the model-bill ambition

### Is this only for Utah?

No. The bill is **written as a model bill**, meant to be passed in every state. Utah is the proving ground because that is where the principal author lives and has standing to push. Every substantive provision is drafted to be portable: the doctrinal abrogations track federal Supreme Court immunity cases that apply nationwide; the substantive offenses describe government misconduct patterns that occur in every state; the Citizen Council enforcement mechanism is built on the universal principle that prosecutors cannot be the sole gatekeepers of accountability over their own profession.

### How would another state adopt this?

Fork the repository, swap the few Utah-specific anchors (Title 76 cross-references, Utah Constitution citations, Utah county-and-municipal-structure references) for the equivalent provisions of the adopting state's law, and present the resulting bill to a sponsoring legislator. The architecture does not need to change. The substantive offenses, the immunity abrogations, the Citizen Council mechanism, and the mens rea framework all work the same way in any state.

### What if my state's constitution is different?

Most state constitutions contain similar declarations of rights, similar separation-of-powers architectures, and similar provisions on the rights of the accused. The bill is drafted to be compatible with the general structure that state constitutions share. State-specific tailoring may be required for unique provisions, but the core architecture is portable.

### Can I add my own state's specific concerns?

Yes. The repository is intentionally open to citizen development. State-specific patterns of abuse — particular cases, particular agencies, particular industries — can be added as state-specific provisions or community guides. The bill is designed to be added to, not just adapted from.

### Will the bill be introduced in the Utah Legislature?

The bill exists in fully drafted form and is intended to be introduced in substantially this form. Introduction requires a sponsoring legislator. Citizen support is how that sponsoring relationship is built — the more citizens of Utah who support the bill, the easier it becomes for a legislator to take the political step of introduction.

### What if my legislator opposes the bill?

That is, regrettably, predictable. The bill addresses an immunity architecture that benefits a substantial constituency within both parties. The path forward where legislators oppose is the path that any citizen-written reform has always required: citizen pressure, public attention, primary challenges to legislators who openly defend the immunity architecture, and a long campaign of building the political conditions under which the bill becomes politically inevitable. The bill is not designed to be easy to pass; it is designed to be right to pass.

## 13. Common objections and concerns

### Won't this cause officials to leave their jobs?

The empirical record on accountability reforms suggests the opposite of the predicted exodus: when departments adopt serious accountability measures, they tend to retain officers who want to do the work professionally and lose officers who would prefer to work without oversight. The bill is calibrated to reach misconduct, not lawful work — and the officials worth retaining are precisely the ones who have nothing to fear from a bill that targets recklessness, intent, and malice.

### Will good cops be afraid to do their jobs?

A police officer who exercises reasonable care, acts within lawful authority, and does not engage in obviously wrongful conduct (theft, fabrication, retaliation, etc.) has nothing to fear from this bill. The "afraid to do their job" concern is, on close examination, almost always a concern about losing the freedom to act *outside* lawful authority. That freedom is precisely what the bill is designed to end — and ending it does not impair the exercise of lawful authority.

### Will judges refuse to rule on hard cases?

Judges who make reasoned legal decisions in good faith, within their jurisdiction, on the law as it exists, have nothing to fear from this bill. The bill reaches malicious, corrupt, ultra vires, or grossly negligent judicial conduct — not the ordinary difficulty of judging hard cases. The "afraid to rule" concern, like the "afraid to do their job" concern, on close examination, almost always reduces to a desire to retain immunity for conduct that should never have been immunized.

### Will prosecutors decline to prosecute violent criminals to avoid liability?

A prosecutor who decides in good faith not to charge a particular case faces no liability under this bill. The bill reaches **selective non-prosecution** — the prosecutor's refusal to charge a friend, fellow official, or political ally for an offense that the prosecutor would charge any ordinary citizen for. Good-faith prosecutorial discretion within the bounds of equal application of the law is unaffected.

### What stops vindictive citizens from weaponizing this against officials?

The bill includes meaningful safeguards: Citizen Councils require twelve or more citizens to act in concert (no single complainant can drive a case); substantive offenses require defined mens rea (recklessness or higher in most cases); conflict-of-interest rules exclude personal vendettas; the same evidentiary standards apply that apply in any other Utah proceeding; criminal liability for knowingly false complaints under generally applicable Utah perjury and false-reporting law; and the ordinary due-process protections of the accused remain in full force.

### Won't this cost taxpayers a fortune?

Most of the bill's mechanisms operate on existing infrastructure — the County Prosecutor's Office, the Sheriff's Office, the Governor's Office, the existing court system. Citizen Councils are self-organized and don't require funding. Where the State pays out civil judgments under the bill, the bill expressly forbids using state funds to indemnify the individual government actor — meaning the bill's deterrent effect actually reduces the long-term cost of misconduct settlements that taxpayers currently absorb.

### What if the bill is too sweeping — too many provisions, too much scope?

The bill is comprehensive because the immunity architecture it addresses is comprehensive. Trimming it to "just" qualified immunity, or "just" judicial immunity, or "just" police misconduct, would leave the other gaps unaddressed and the underlying asymmetry intact. The bill is intentionally written as a complete instrument so that citizens who adopt it adopt the whole architecture of accountability, not a partial fix that leaves loopholes for the next form of misconduct to migrate into.

### What if a future legislature waters this down or repeals it?

That risk applies to any statute. The bill includes a Section 15 entrenchment provision that addresses constitutional-amendment-level protection where state law allows it, and citizens who succeed in adopting the bill in their state have a strong political base from which to defend it. Repeal would itself become a politically costly act — that is part of what political accountability for elected officials is supposed to mean.

### Won't this politicize the courts?

The bill addresses misconduct, not policy disagreement. Reasonable, good-faith judicial decisions are not reachable. The bill is not a mechanism for one side of a political dispute to punish the other side's judges — and the mens rea and substantive-elements requirements are calibrated to prevent it from being used that way. The "politicization" concern, on close examination, often reduces to a concern that any judicial accountability is politicization — which the bill respectfully rejects.

### Is this really going to work?

The bill is an instrument. Whether it works depends on whether citizens engage with it — read it, support it, push legislators to introduce it, serve on Citizen Councils when called to, and use it where it applies to their own circumstances. The bill is not a magic solution; it is a tool. The honest answer to "will it work" is: it will work to the extent citizens use it. That is true of every law worth passing, and it is true of this one.
