# Veterans, Moral Injury, and Why This Bill Will Reduce the Veteran Suicide Count

> **If you are a veteran in crisis or you are worried about a veteran:** the **Veterans Crisis Line** is available 24/7 — dial **988**, then press **1**, or text **838255**. The line is confidential, free, and staffed by VA-trained responders, many of whom are veterans themselves. You do not need to be enrolled in VA care to use it.

**Operation Checkmate will reduce the veteran suicide count.** It will do so by addressing a recognized, under-treated driver of veteran suicide — moral injury — in a way that no existing legal or therapeutic intervention does. The clinical literature on military mental health has documented the mechanism for fifteen years. The civic instrument that would meet that mechanism at structural scale has not, until now, existed. This bill is that instrument.

---

## 1. The crisis

Approximately **17 veterans die by suicide every day** in the United States. The rate runs **roughly 1.5 to 2 times the rate of the comparable non-veteran adult population** even after adjustment for age and sex (VA Office of Mental Health and Suicide Prevention, *National Veteran Suicide Prevention Annual Report*, recent editions). The earlier "22 a day" figure came from a 2012 VA report using a more limited dataset (Kemp & Bossarte, 2012); the modern methodology produced a lower headline number, but the underlying conclusion is unchanged: veteran suicide occurs at substantially elevated rates compared to the general population.

The clinical and policy response has been substantial — the Veterans Crisis Line, hundreds of suicide-prevention coordinators across VA medical centers, evidence-based PTSD treatment protocols, telehealth expansion, peer-support programs, lethal-means counseling, the VA Mission Act community-care expansion. Yet the suicide rate has not meaningfully declined over the period in which these interventions have scaled (VA OMHSP annual reports, 2018–present).

That gap is itself a finding. The interventions reach the wounds they were designed to reach. They do not, on the evidence, reach the wound that is killing a large share of these veterans.

## 2. The wound the existing landscape does not reach: moral injury

The dominant clinical paradigm for veteran mental health has been built around fear-extinction treatments for PTSD. Those treatments work for the conditions they were designed to treat. They do not reach a distinct, equally serious category of post-service distress called **moral injury** — and the most rigorous review of the evidence makes the gap plain. Steenkamp, Litz, Hoge, and Marmar (2015), in *JAMA*, found that even gold-standard exposure-based therapies leave a substantial fraction of veterans symptomatic at follow-up, with effect sizes lower than those reported in civilian PTSD populations (Steenkamp et al., 2015). The military population carries a moral-injury load that the standard fear-extinction protocols are not designed to reach.

The term *moral injury* was coined by clinical psychiatrist Jonathan Shay in his work with Vietnam combat veterans, defined as the wound that occurs when **"there has been a betrayal of what's right, by someone who holds legitimate authority, in a high-stakes situation"** (Shay, 1994; Shay, 2014). The construct was operationalized for modern clinical use by Brett Litz and colleagues in a foundational 2009 paper in *Clinical Psychology Review*, where they defined moral injury as the lasting psychological, biological, spiritual, behavioral, and social impact of **"perpetrating, failing to prevent, bearing witness to, or learning about acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations"** (Litz et al., 2009). The VA's own National Center for PTSD now acknowledges moral injury as a distinct construct requiring distinct clinical attention (Maguen & Litz, *PTSD Research Quarterly*, 2012, published by the VA National Center for PTSD).

The differences between PTSD and moral injury are clinically established:

- **PTSD is a fear-based disorder.** The core symptoms are hyperarousal, intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, and negative alterations in cognition and mood, all organized around a traumatic event experienced as life-threatening. Fear is the gravitational center.

- **Moral injury is a meaning-based wound.** The core experience is guilt, shame, betrayal, loss of meaning, loss of trust in legitimate authority, loss of trust in oneself, and a corrosive sense that the moral framework one operated under has been broken. Meaning is the gravitational center.

- **They co-occur often but are not the same wound, and they do not respond to the same treatments.** Drescher and colleagues (2011) documented the inadequacy of standard PTSD treatments for the moral-injury component. The field has subsequently developed dedicated moral-injury treatments — Adaptive Disclosure Therapy (Litz et al.) and the Impact of Killing intervention (Maguen et al.) being the two most studied — though they remain far less widely available than the established PTSD protocols.

Critically, **moral injury independently elevates suicide risk after controlling for PTSD severity**. Wisco and colleagues, drawing on the National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study, found that exposure to moral-injury events in U.S. combat veterans was independently associated with adverse mental-health outcomes — including suicidality — after controlling for PTSD severity (Wisco et al., *Depression and Anxiety*, 2017). The mechanism is direct: a person whose moral framework has been broken, whose sense of meaning has been corroded, and who cannot reconcile what they witnessed or what they were asked to do with who they understood themselves to be, is a person at elevated risk of concluding that no future state of affairs will repair the wound.

Rita Nakashima Brock and Gabriella Lettini's *Soul Repair: Recovering from Moral Injury After War* (2012) articulated what repair actually requires: **community recognition of the wound**, **a path by which the broken meaning can be rebuilt**, and **opportunities for the wounded person to act in ways that restore moral agency**. Litz, Drescher, and the modern moral-injury literature converge on the same three elements (Litz et al., 2009; Drescher et al., 2011). Moral injury is not the kind of wound that medication and symptom-protocols alone can heal.

## 3. The constitutional moral injury that veterans carry into civilian life

Every service member, on entry to the Armed Forces, takes an oath. The exact wording differs slightly between officers and enlisted personnel, but the central object of the oath is identical: **the Constitution of the United States, against all enemies, foreign and domestic.** The oath is not to a president, not to a party, not to a deployment, not even to a commanding officer. It is to the Constitution.

That oath is reinforced, throughout the service member's career, by life under the **Uniform Code of Military Justice** — a comprehensive disciplinary code in which the foundational principle is that **rank does not exempt anyone from the law**. Service members live, sometimes for decades, inside a regime that takes the constitutional architecture seriously enough to enforce it against generals.

Then they separate. The oath does not expire — service members consistently describe it as a permanent commitment. But the service member is no longer under the UCMJ, no longer in a chain of command, no longer in any structural role that gives the oath operative civilian meaning. The service member is a civilian, navigating a civilian government that — as the body of this bill documents — operates effectively *without* a comparable disciplinary code. **Judges** are shielded by absolute judicial immunity from accountability for malicious or grossly injurious conduct (*Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. 335; *Stump v. Sparkman*, 435 U.S. 349). **Prosecutors** are shielded by absolute prosecutorial immunity even where they knowingly use false testimony to convict (*Imbler v. Pachtman*, 424 U.S. 409). **Officers** are shielded by qualified immunity from civil liability for conduct that would not survive a moment's scrutiny under the UCMJ (*Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800; *Jessop v. City of Fresno*, 936 F.3d 937). And the citizen — including the veteran citizen — has had no operative state-level instrument by which the oath to the Constitution can be acted on.

This is **constitutional moral injury**, and its content maps cleanly onto the established moral-injury literature. There has been a betrayal of what's right by people holding legitimate authority — exactly Shay's foundational formulation (Shay, 1994; 2014). The service member has witnessed (often, has experienced firsthand through VA care, through court interactions, through encounters with civilian agencies after separation) acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations — exactly the Litz definition (Litz et al., 2009). The meaning framework that organized the service member's adult life — *I swore to defend the Constitution, and I am bound to that oath* — has been left without an operative civilian form to act in. The Constitution is violated, daily, by the government the veteran served, and the veteran has no instrument to defend it.

This explains a pattern the standard moral-injury formulation under-reaches: veterans who never deployed to combat, who carry no fear-based PTSD signature, who completed their service honorably, and who nevertheless describe in language that maps directly onto the moral-injury literature a loss of meaning and a loss of moral agency in their post-service civilian lives. The wound is real. The cause is structural. And the existing treatment landscape — built around fear-extinction protocols for PTSD — does not reach it.

## 4. How this bill heals it

Operation Checkmate addresses constitutional moral injury at its structural source. The mechanisms map directly onto what the moral-injury literature has identified as the elements of repair.

**The bill gives the oath an operative civilian form.** A veteran who took the oath to the Constitution and who watches that Constitution be violated by the government they served, with no instrument to defend it, is in a meaning-based bind that the clinical literature would recognize as untreated. The bill creates a state-level instrument — Citizen Councils, criminal abrogation of the immunity architecture, the substantive offenses of Sections 1, 2, 5, and 17 — by which a citizen, including a veteran citizen, can actually act on the oath. The oath becomes, again, something that can be lived rather than something that can only be remembered. This is the **agency restoration** that Litz, Brock, Lettini, and Drescher have all identified as central to moral repair (Litz et al., 2009; Brock & Lettini, 2012; Drescher et al., 2011).

**The bill builds, at the state level, the disciplinary regime the veteran already lives under.** The UCMJ is the disciplinary code the service member was governed by in service. The bill's premise — that the civilian government, when it exercises power over citizens, should be subject to a comparable code rather than to the immunity architecture that has substituted for one — gives the veteran a civilian regime that is structurally recognizable. The cognitive dissonance of moving from a regime in which rank does not exempt anyone, into a regime in which rank effectively exempts everyone, is what generates a large share of the constitutional moral injury. The bill collapses that dissonance in the jurisdiction that adopts it.

**The bill reaches the institutions whose mistreatment of veterans has produced documented harm.** The VA, where veterans have repeatedly described bureaucratic indifference, denial of benefits, denial of care, and — in the worst-documented cases — patient mortality directly attributable to administrative failure, is itself a state actor when it operates within a state. Federal-officer status is not, under §14.7 of this Act, a shield from state criminal accountability in Utah. The bill's provisions on retaliation (§17.2), institutional fraud (§17.6), and family-separation under official process (§1.1.19, §17.9I) reach the kinds of conduct that veterans have repeatedly identified as compounding rather than alleviating the moral injuries of service. A veteran who has been mistreated by the VA gains, under this bill, the instrument to hold the responsible actors to account.

**The bill provides community recognition of the wound.** Citizen Councils are, by design, a community-recognition mechanism — twelve or more citizens, convened jointly, gathering evidence and rendering a finding. For a veteran whose moral-injury wound has been invisible to civilian therapists, civilian family members, civilian institutions, and even the VA itself, the existence of a structural mechanism by which fellow citizens recognize the wrong is — on the Brock and Lettini account — itself a part of repair (Brock & Lettini, 2012). The community is not asked to share the wound. It is asked to recognize that the wound occurred, and to participate in the response. That is what the moral-injury repair literature has consistently identified as central, and that is what the existing landscape has not, until now, structurally provided.

These four mechanisms are the structural conditions under which moral-injury treatment can take hold and meaning can be rebuilt. The clinical interventions remain necessary and should be supported and expanded. The bill is the civic instrument that — in a way no clinical intervention can — addresses the underlying meaning-based wound at its structural source rather than at its symptomatic surface.

The clinical literature has documented the mechanism for fifteen years. The civic instrument has not existed. **Adopting this bill is, on the evidence, the single most consequential structural intervention available to reduce the share of veteran suicide that is driven by constitutional moral injury.**

## 5. What veterans can do, today

The bill, even before adoption, is a citizen instrument. A veteran reading this document can do several things that the moral-injury repair literature would recognize as constructive:

- **Read the bill.** The full text is in [`bill_proposal.md`](bill_proposal.md). The PDF is at [https://github.com/killerdevildog/checkmate/releases/latest](https://github.com/killerdevildog/checkmate/releases/latest) and can be uploaded to ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, or Gemini for a guided read.
- **Sign the petition.** Email **citizens.checkmate@gmail.com** with subject `CHECKMATE` and your first and last name and zip code. Signatures are how citizens demonstrate the bill has the support to be introduced.
- **Comment on it.** [Open an issue on GitHub](https://github.com/killerdevildog/checkmate/issues) with anything you would add, change, sharpen, or push back against. Veteran perspective on what real accountability looks like — informed by the disciplinary regime you lived under — is rare and valuable.
- **Talk about it.** With fellow veterans, with veterans' organizations, with your representatives. The model-bill ambition is to see it adopted in every state. That happens through citizen pressure, which happens through citizens — including veterans, whose voice on this subject carries particular weight — being willing to speak.

If you or a veteran you know is in crisis: **the Veterans Crisis Line is 988, press 1**. The line is available 24/7, free, confidential, and staffed by people who understand what they are listening to. You do not need to be enrolled in VA care. You do not need to use your real name. You do not need a plan or a reason. The line exists for the call you are considering making.
